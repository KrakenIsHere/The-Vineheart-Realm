# Narrative style

Use this guidance for narrative chapters in **6. Stories**. Rules and history notes follow the vault's plain voice conventions. Record each story's viewpoint, narrator's voice, tone, and special formats in that story's folder.

## Flow & viewpoint

Use fluid narration with varied sentence lengths. Connect related actions, sensations, and reactions so the reader can follow a movement through its consequence. Keep short sentences and paragraph breaks when they serve tension, emphasis, or humor. Avoid a repeated sequence of isolated statements.

Stay within the viewpoint character's knowledge. Let characters notice, infer, misunderstand, and learn. Give observations a reason to matter in the scene. Quieter moments should still have physical presence.

## Thoughts & dialogue

Italicize direct inner thoughts. Keep spoken dialogue in quotation marks and ordinary narration in plain text.

Dialogue belongs to the scene. Include meaningful movement, hesitation, or a bodily reaction when it adds to the exchange. A frightened statement should carry the speaker's fear into their next action. Choose reactions that affect the moment; every line does not need its own gesture.

## Action & surroundings

Let dangerous moments unfold through what the character notices, anticipates, and tries to do. Show the movement that succeeds or fails, the physical consequence, and the next response. Give fear and uncertainty weight through choices, mistakes, hesitation, pain, and interrupted plans.

Establish the viewpoint character's position and the important exits before movement. Anchor left and right to that viewpoint. Show turns, changes in level, and transitions between rooms. Describe an unfamiliar mechanism through its visible parts and their position relative to the character. Introduce a part before referring to it by a short label, and distinguish nearby parts that serve different purposes.

Doors, gates, and moving machinery make appropriate sounds. Damage has consequences beyond the point of impact: falling material, altered water flow, strained supports, or disrupted machinery. People familiar with a place should notice when it behaves differently.

## Humor & meaning

When humor suits a scene, let jokes grow from the characters' circumstances, expectations, and reactions. Dry humor works well at the end of a paragraph. Keep danger and injury meaningful when the scene turns funny.

Let an action or observation carry its meaning. Add reflection when it contributes a new thought, feeling, or decision. Avoid explaining the same implication several times. Preserve the character's voice when tightening a passage.

## Chapter pacing

Around **4,000–7,000 words** is a guide, not a hard limit. Chapters can differ in length. Choose stopping points by the scene's arc and the reader's need for a pause.

Split a long chapter at a useful transition. Keep complete scenes together and preserve the intended chronology. A chapter break alone does not imply elapsed time, rest, or recovery.

Name a chapter for its main thread or a meaningful line. A small event idea does not need to become the whole chapter's subject or title.

## Chapter layout

Give each chapter one visible heading with its number and title. Build prose paragraphs around one continuing action, observation, or reflection. Start a new paragraph when the focus changes, rather than after every sentence or small beat.

Start a new paragraph when the speaker changes. Keep a speaker's connected speech and actions together when attribution stays clear. Inner thoughts can stay within the surrounding paragraph when they belong to the same moment. Use standalone lines for emphasis, tension, or humor.

Use `* * *` for scene breaks at meaningful transitions in time, location, or the scene's focus. Moving through a doorway alone does not require a scene break. When using reading links, keep them below a separate divider after the chapter's final paragraph.

## Word choices

Use **American English** and concrete, familiar words. Avoid epic-trailer phrasing and stacked adjectives.

Choose descriptive words that fit the actual sensation or condition. Make cold, pain, discomfort, danger, or relief clear through the description or the character's response. Dry understatement and figurative language can remain when the context makes their meaning clear; they should not leave the reader unsure what happened or how it felt.

Avoid **lay** and **trousers** in story prose. Use **pants** and rephrase positions or actions naturally.

Use **stone** for construction, worked material, and individual small pieces; **rock** for rough natural formations and unworked material. These terms overlap, and size alone does not decide. Use **rubble** or **chunks of stone** for broken masonry. Reserve **block** and **blocks** for deliberately cut or shaped stone, such as a block of granite. Name objects by their function when it makes the description clearer.

## Continuity

Keep actions and consequences consistent with the story's established abilities, limitations, history, and physical surroundings. Record the specific rules and supporting lore in that story's folder. Track where carried objects are placed and when they are picked up again. Show the transfer when it affects lighting, free hands, movement, or another character's reaction.

## Feedback & revisions

Treat feedback forwarded from readers or other people as suggestions to evaluate. Consider what it identifies, whether the proposed change fits the story, and any effects on voice, pacing, or continuity. Explain the recommendation and offer wording when useful.

When feedback warrants a revision, identify the underlying issue and check the other chapters in the same story for it, including different wording that causes the same confusion. Assess each occurrence in context. Preserve short sentences, paragraph breaks, and physical reactions that serve the scene, along with the story's voice, spatial clarity, and established rules.

Forwarded feedback alone does not authorize chapter edits. The author's own requests determine which revisions to make.
