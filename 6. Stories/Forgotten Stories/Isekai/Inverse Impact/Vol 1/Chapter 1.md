A monster in another world died because I patted it on the head. I did not punch it, stab it, or blast it apart with legendary fire magic. I already knew a gentle touch could break stone. The problem was getting close enough to use it without losing my hand.

The wolf's front legs were pinned between two fallen beams, but its mouth still reached the rusty sword I held in front of it. Teeth closed on the blade with a scrape that traveled into my sore hand. I set my feet wider and tried not to pull back. Wrestling with it would only bring its teeth closer.

My free hand hovered above its lowered head. I could feel its breath against my wrist, and each movement of its jaw made my fingers curl. I opened them again, reached over the teeth, and rested my palm against the fur. Then I gave it the smallest nudge I could manage, away from me.

"Good doggy."

The **Hellfang Wolf** disappeared.

There was a sound like thunder trapped in a cupboard. The rotten beams burst apart as the beast tore free and went backward through a stone wall whose mortar had been falling out long before I arrived. My sword clattered at my feet. I ducked behind my raised forearm, coughing as dust rolled through the hole and chunks of stone tumbled into the chamber beyond.

When I could see again, a tuft of fur drifted past my face and settled among the rubble. My left palm was still open.

I stayed where I was until the echoes stopped. A moment earlier, the wolf's shoulders had been as high as my waist, and its teeth had looked entirely capable of removing the hand I had just used. Now I could see into the next room. It had acquired a wolf. I was in no hurry to ask whether it wanted one.

I stared at my hand. My hand stared back, offering no explanation for its behavior.

"That was supposed to stop the wolf," I whispered. *What exactly are the rules here?*

The blue window unfolded in front of my face again.

I flinched at how close it appeared, then held my hand against my chest while I read. Unlike the marks on the dungeon walls, the writing was in Japanese, just as it had been in the summoning hall. That remained its one considerate feature.

> **Unique Skill:** Inverse Impact
>
> The less physical force the bearer intentionally applies, the greater the resulting impact.
>
> **Warning:** Excessive tenderness may cause catastrophic results.

I read the warning three times. The force rule was familiar. I had tested it yesterday, at considerable expense to people who owned a training yard, and survived skeletons by using it. The part about "excessive tenderness" was new. Apparently the window had decided that breaking things was no longer embarrassing enough without a description of my technique.

"Excessive tenderness?"

A second window slid into place beneath the first.

> **Contact recorded:** Hellfang Wolf.
>
> **Hit Points:** 0 / 37.
> **Condition:** Dead.
>
> **Result:** Lethal.

"No, that is not the problem!"

My voice traveled through the broken wall. Before its echo faded, a roar came back from somewhere farther away. I covered my mouth and turned toward the hole, listening for the scrape of claws on stone.

"Sorry," I whispered. The word hardly cleared my fingers. I took a step back.

While leaning back to listen, I reached for the wall with my free hand. My fingers found a stone with crumbling mortar around its edges. I could have left my hand there. Instead, I gave it a tiny, careful push to steady myself. The stone split, and a crack ran through the loose joints above it.

I pulled away as dust sprinkled onto my shoulder. My fingers curled against my chest, and I watched the crack until the last loose grain stopped falling. Nothing else broke. I moved my feet instead of reaching for something to steady me.

*Fantastic. Even an apology requires somewhere safe to put my hands.*

The beams had bought me one chance to make contact while its teeth were occupied. I had meant to knock it away. I had no reliable way to choose how far. "Good doggy" had been an instruction to my own shoulders; dogs at home did not make me lock every muscle in my body.

There was nothing to be gained by checking the rubble. I backed out of the room, stepping around the loose stones, and found a patch of floor that had not yet developed a personal grievance against me.

I sat there until the shaking in my knees became small enough to ignore, turning my left hand over as though the dangerous part might be visible from the other side. It looked ordinary, with dirt beneath the nails and a shallow scratch across the thumb. Yesterday, that hand had held a sandwich. I would have liked to return to that level of responsibility.

Once the dust thinned, I crouched beside the doorway to retrieve the sword. My hand stopped just above the hilt when something settled beyond the broken wall. I waited, then picked it up. Two tooth marks crossed the blade and one edge had acquired a notch, but the hilt was intact. I wiped it on a clean patch of my pants before sliding it back into the scabbard.

Through the broken wall, I could see a red eye among the rubble. It remained open and motionless. I had needed the wolf to stop trying to eat me. Now that it had, I wished there were a way to choose a result somewhere between being eaten and whatever I had just done.

A pebble clicking down the rubble pile brought me to my feet, making my bruised ribs complain. Although it was only settling stone, I waited for breath, claws, or the scrape of another sword. The blue lamps made the corridor bright enough to see my own shadow, but left every doorway dark beyond its first few feet.

*One room at a time. Get through this one, then worry about the next.*

It was the sort of advice I would have resented from anyone else. Being alone had reduced the available instructors.

Testing my weight on both feet revealed that the cloth around my left sock had shifted, exposing skin to the cold floor. I sat again to fix it, holding the fabric and tucking its loose edge beneath the wrap without making anything happen. Concentrating on that small, ordinary task helped my breathing settle. There were still things I could do safely. The problem was remembering them while something with teeth argued for my attention.

---

Yesterday, I had been **Haruto Minase**, twenty-two years old, underpaid *whenever anyone was paying me*, and carrying fourteen hundred yen in my wallet when I fell asleep on a commuter train. There had been no truck, no goddess, and no heroic sacrifice. I had taken a nap, which seemed an unreasonable basis for everything that was happening.

People in robes had hoped to summon a champion. They got someone who processed invoices. My ordinary abilities had disappointed them; my 'extraordinary' skill had damaged the palace. They called what happened afterward temporary relocation. I called it being banished before dinner, though the paperwork was apparently very firm on the distinction.

Since then, I had been **robbed**, **chased** by slimes, and **dropped** into the **Hanging Halls** through a hole I had made worse while trying not to fall down it. The way back was buried. I had counted twenty-three downward stairways, then realized several might have been the same stairway approached from different directions. I stopped keeping a floor count before it became another reason to panic.

The sword at my hip was not the one the palace had given me. The bandits owned that one now. I had found this sword on a rack beside an empty guardroom, after spending several corridors insisting I did not need a weapon. Rust spotted the blade, but it had a hilt I could grip, which put it ahead of every bone I had considered carrying instead.

I also had one shoe, cloth tied around my other foot, and a phone with a cracked screen. *A miracle that the fall didn't do more damage.* The last time I checked, its battery was at eighteen percent. I had switched it off. If rescue depended on someone calling me, there were already several larger problems with the plan.

The blue windows stayed at the edge of my vision as I stepped into the corridor, following every turn of my head.

*If they know what that monster was, perhaps they know the way out.*

"Can you tell me where the exit is?"

When the writing stayed exactly as it was, I tried a request that sounded more like something a window might understand.

"Show me my status."

The wolf's assessment faded beneath a larger window bearing my name.

> **Name:** Haruto Minase.
>
> **Level:** 2.
> **Class:** None.
> **Hit Points:** 5 / 7.
> **Armor Class:** 12 (unarmored).
> **Spell Slots:** None.
>
> **Condition:** Injured, Fatigued.
> **Occupation:** None.
> **Designation:** Catastrophic Pacifist.
> **Title:** Gentle Hands.

The level and armor numbers were unchanged since the summoning hall, but my hit points had fallen from the seven I remembered before anyone started giving me things to punch. Now I had five.

I looked at the cut beneath my sleeve, then at the number. It did not tell me how many more mistakes I could survive. Five sounded like a quantity I should stop spending.

There was no class and no supply of spells tucked away somewhere. The hand that broke stone belonged to someone wearing a work shirt for protection. I had known that already. Seeing the assessment in writing made it harder to pretend the situation would improve on its own.

Then I read the new designation.

"Catastrophic Pacifist?"

The window remained politely blue.

"And please stop calling it Gentle Hands. That sounds like something you book an appointment for."

Whatever was writing these messages knew my name, recognized a monster I had never heard of, and had no apparent interest in the one piece of information that might keep me alive. A search around the window's edge revealed no customer-support option either.

The passage divided ahead. One branch climbed beneath an arch with a cracked corner. The other sloped down beside a pair of bronze rails. Both had blue lamps. Both had the same damp smell. I spent a while examining them for the kind of obvious difference a person in a story ought to notice.

There were marks cut into the wall beside the upper branch. I recognized a square, three short lines, and something shaped like a hook. They could have meant EXIT, STORAGE, or PLEASE DO NOT FEED THE WOLF. The translation spell had given me speech. It had apparently left signs as an exercise.

I set three loose pebbles in a triangle beside the junction. Carrying and placing them was harmless. I had learned enough about my hands to avoid pressing them into the floor.

The arrangement looked deliberate. If I came back, I would recognize it. If someone else came through, perhaps they would realize an intelligent creature had been here. I moved one pebble to make the triangle more even, then decided I had spent quite enough time advertising my intelligence.

I chose the upper passage. Beyond the first bend, it widened into a landing at the foot of a broad stone stairway. The stairs climbed along the right-hand wall, with cold air drifting down from above. That seemed more promising than going deeper.

A blade scraped against stone ahead of me.

Three skeletal soldiers emerged beneath a blue lamp at the far end of the landing. Rusty armor hung from their ribs, green light burned in their sockets, and all three carried swords. I had met enough skeletons by then to know they did not respond to reasonable explanations. Unfortunately, knowing what to do and keeping my hand steady while a sword came toward my face were different skills.

The first came at me with its sword raised. I drew my borrowed weapon, fumbling the last inch clear of the scabbard, and tried to bring the edge toward its skull without swinging. Then its blade filled the space in front of my face.

I flinched. My shoulder tightened, my grip locked, and the careful movement became a frantic swing before I could stop it.

The blade rang against its skull. The skull remained a skull. The skeleton tilted its head as though trying to understand the point of the demonstration.

"I meant to tap you," I said, trying to draw the blade back between us.

Its free hand came past the sword. Knuckles struck my mouth before I had room to duck, and I tasted blood. The hit-point number at the edge of my vision dropped to four.

My heel caught a fallen stone. I went backward and landed hard on my hip, losing the sword as I tried to keep my head off the floor. I reached for it, then pulled my hand back when three sets of boots entered the space in front of me. I needed air. I needed to get out from under the blades.

*I know what to do. My hand needs to stop arguing.*

Beside me stood the base of a broken statue, its carved feet all that remained at the foot of the stairs. Two skeletons approached while the third waited behind them with its sword raised. They were slow enough for me to follow the blades.

I started to reach for my sword, but it was between me and the skeleton on the right. Getting it back would mean putting my face at sword height.

The nearest blade struck the carved ankle above me with a crack. I ducked so sharply that a chip fell inside my collar, cold against the sweat at my neck. There was no time to shake it out. I crawled around the base, dragging my wrapped foot through dust and stopping for half a breath when my hip protested. The skeleton's boots followed. I moved again before its sword cleared the statue.

Its knee joint scraped the base as the second skeleton crowded behind it, trying to get around the same obstruction. Neither seemed concerned about giving the other room. I had worked beside people like that. Usually they carried coffee rather than swords.

I watched the first one's hands. When it raised the blade, its elbow left a small gap beneath the sword arm. If I could get close enough, I would only need a moment. I would also need to be right about which way the sword was going.

My left hand kept closing into a fist. I spread the fingers, rested them on my own knee, and waited for it to swing its blade. The window stayed in the corner of my vision, reporting that I was injured and fatigued. I had already gathered that information.

*Wait until it has to move its feet.*

Rubble was piled unevenly beside the base. I shifted toward it, making the skeleton change direction. The move hurt. It still felt better than giving it a clear path to my head.

I edged around the base, keeping low enough that the stone still covered my head. The first skeleton followed with its sword near its knee. Its boot caught between two stones as it stepped over the rubble, and the blade dipped while it looked down.

I brought my good foot beneath me. My hip hurt when I rose, and for a moment I was certain I would be too slow. Its forehead came within reach. I lifted my hand before it could look up.

"No hard feelings."

I flicked it.

The impact cracked through the corridor. The skull shot backward, dragging the helmet with it, and struck the skeleton immediately behind it. Armor and bones crashed into the third. By the time the pieces reached the far wall, all three skeletons had become a problem for whoever swept the floor. A helmet struck a loose brick, knocked it out, and let cold air into the passage.

Somewhere on the other side, something enormous screamed.

I dropped behind the base again, listening to the pieces strike the floor. The scream beyond the wall lasted longer than the clatter. I held my breath until it ended, then let the air out through a mouth that tasted of blood. My lip was bleeding, my hip hurt, and whatever had made that sound now knew something was happening here.

"That was a flick," I told my finger.

A treasure chest dropped from the ceiling.

It struck the floor beside the scattered bones, bounced once, and shed a cloud of dust. From behind the statue, I looked up at a storage shelf fixed high on the far wall, above the spot where the skeletons had struck. Most of it was still attached; the part that had held the chest was now beside it on the floor.

"Why was that in the ceiling?"

The latch broke off. The lid swung open, revealing a black cloak, a pair of gloves, and a silver ring. I waited for teeth, poison, or an angry little person to emerge. None did. I left the shelter of the statue and picked my way through the bones to the chest. It was already the most welcoming thing I had met underground.

When I reached toward the gloves, a new window appeared.

> **Gloves of Delicate Control**
>
> Dampens minor unintended impacts.
>
> **Deliberate force remains dangerous.**

I almost cried. Not because the warning was reassuring, but because the sentence before it contained the possibility that something might help me.

"Thank God. Any available deity of such generosity!"

Nothing exploded when I lifted the gloves by their cuffs. They were thin and dark, stitched with silver thread, and fitted over my hands once I worked off Ordan's leather pair, stiff with dirt and blood. A faint tingle moved across my knuckles, then faded.

Still nothing exploded. I sat beside the chest, with a broken crate against my back, and hugged myself in relief.

The old gloves rested in my lap. Ordan had given them to me at the palace doors with the expression of a man who knew exactly how little help they would be. One cuff was split, and dried blood had stiffened the lining. I folded them and tucked them inside my jacket. They had kept enough splinters out of my hands to deserve better than being left beside a broken chest.

The new pair fit closely enough for me to bend every finger without the leather bunching at the joints. I turned my hands beneath the lamp, following the silver stitches over my knuckles and into the seams at my wrists, looking for a buckle, a dial, or anything that would tell me how much protection I had.

The window offered the same two sentences.

*Minor unintended impacts. That is a very small promise.*

It was still a promise. Nobody at the palace had managed even that.

As I shifted to stand, I lost my balance and pushed lightly against the stone ledge beside me. The silver stitches warmed across my palm. A chip snapped off, but the rest of the ledge stayed where it was. Yesterday, a similarly careful push had broken a great deal more stone.

"Right. Less leaning."

I could hold things. I could rest my hands. Pushing to keep myself upright was a different matter, and the gloves had turned this particular mistake into a chip instead of a hole. I would have preferred a railing I could trust, but I was prepared to be grateful in installments.

The cloak smelled of cedar and old cupboards. As I lifted it from the chest, the writing changed.

> **Traveler's Cloak**
>
> Keeps the wearer dry and improves personal insulation.

Wrapping it around myself made the draft against my back considerably less interesting. When I picked up the silver ring, another window appeared.

> **Ring of Mild Warmth**
>
> Provides a steady, gentle warmth to the wearer.
>
> **Does not prevent hypothermia.**

I slid it onto my right index finger. Warmth spread through my cold hand, faint but welcome. Neither window promised an exit, a meal, or replacement footwear. I kept them anyway. At least they had a purpose I understood.

*It is not cold enough for me to worry about hypothermia anyway. But staying warm is a plus.*

With the new equipment on, I walked back to the statue at the foot of the stairs. My sword was still where I had dropped it during the fight. I retrieved it, checked that the scabbard had not come loose, and started up, leaving the chest and the skeletons' remains.

At the top of the stairs, I stopped on a stone walkway overlooking a square shaft. The stairs were behind me; ahead, an iron railing guarded the drop except for a doorway-sized gap beneath a blue lamp.

Immediately beyond the gap, a wooden platform sat inside the shaft with its floor level with the walkway. Its low iron fence had an open gate facing me, close enough for me to step straight onto it. I stayed on the stone.

Two thick iron rails climbed the shaft walls, one on either side of the platform. A heavy chain attached to the platform disappeared into the dark overhead. To its right, a long stone weight hung from a second chain, inside a narrow channel in the wall. Grooves marked where the weight had scraped its guides. Through the space between the platform and the walkway, I could see the shaft continuing down into darkness. This was some kind of goods lift.

A handwheel and a lever were fixed to the railing on the right of the opening, within reach from the walkway. Three marks above the lever were writing. I studied them for several seconds, hoping one would turn into something useful through the strength of my need. They remained marks.

High up the shaft, light shone through a break in the stonework. For a moment, the pale patch looked like sky, but leaning sideways changed the angle enough to reveal its glass cage. Another blue lamp.

*Maybe the platform could get me closer to the surface.*

I moved beside the handwheel and examined the mechanism. A short metal shaft connected it to a large toothed gear, with a catch lodged between two teeth. The rest of the machinery disappeared behind an iron cover. I had no idea what the lever did. I assumed the catch was keeping the platform from dropping at an overly exciting speed.

A dusty painted board rested on the walkway beside the controls. Holding it by the edges beneath the lamp over the gate, I could make out one figure at the wheel and another inside the platform with a load of boxes. Two people.

I set the board down. *Was there a way to use the thing alone?* Perhaps the lever locked the platform at each landing. Perhaps there was another control inside it. I probably couldn't investigate without getting in, or stop it if anything started moving in a way I disliked.

The rim of the handwheel was cold. I closed both hands around it and tried a firm, steady turn, the same sort of pressure that had moved a board normally in the training yard. The mechanism resisted, then scraped through a short movement. Above me, the chain tightened, and the platform rose a few inches. When I stopped, it stayed where it was.

I was beginning to think this might work when something clicked beneath it.

A narrow strip of metal came free beneath the platform. I stopped turning the wheel and watched it fall past the blue light. For a while there was no sound. I had time to imagine standing where that piece had been.

When it finally struck something below, I took my hands off the wheel and stepped back from the opening. The catch held. The stone beneath my feet was solid. I stayed there until I could look at the platform without wanting to move farther away.

Crouching on the walkway let me see beneath the platform's raised edge, where two iron supports were bent and a third had pulled partly out of its joint.

I returned to the railing and leaned just far enough to look up the shaft. The rails continued past the higher lamp, but the landing beside it was choked with rubble. There was no clear opening, and no way to tell whether the platform would reach it before its remaining supports gave up.

I waited a little longer, as though a better answer might become visible. Then I left the platform where it was and stepped away from the controls.

I went back down the stairs, past the fallen chest and the remains of the skeletons. I kept looking over my shoulder. Leaving something that might have been an exit felt worse than finding a wall. But the platform had lost a piece before I put my weight on it. My skill could not hold its floor together, and the gloves would do very little if I fell through it.

I followed the passage back to the junction. My three pebbles were still arranged beside the arch. I added a fourth across the upper side of the triangle. It would remind me that I had tried that way.

The lower passage had water running along it. I could hear it now that things had gone quiet.

---

The trickle in the lower passage became easier to hear as I left the lift behind. I followed the gutter along the wall, still turning at small sounds, until its water gathered in a shallow basin. My first handful scarcely reached my mouth before I wanted another. I drank, then sat with my shoulder beside the basin and let my feet stop carrying me for a while.

Behind me were the failing lift, the wolf, and a series of freshly opened holes that seemed more useful to monsters than to rescuers.

The water tasted of stone and the metal basin. I drank more slowly after the first handful, then wet a corner of my shirt and wiped the dust from my lip. The cut on my forearm had dried into a stiff line beneath the sleeve. I rinsed around it and tied a strip of cloth over it, awkwardly, with my teeth helping more than my right hand.

The window continued to list me as injured, despite the ring's faint warmth in my hand and the cloak keeping the draft off my sweaty back. Both were useful. Neither made the bruise under my ribs go away.

I sat with my feet stretched out and counted what I still had. A sword with wolf damage. A phone I was afraid to turn on. Clothes, a wallet, and the new equipment. The old gloves were tucked inside my jacket. There was no food.

My stomach had been reminding me of that for some time. Water gave it something else to complain about.

Through a narrow doorway beside the basin, I could see shelves, broken baskets, and several lidded pottery jars. The possibility of bread brought me to my feet more quickly than my hip preferred. I would have accepted bread old enough to require a formal apology.

The first jar was empty. The second contained grains stuck together beneath a pale growth. The third had cracked, spilling dark seeds across the shelf. Some had been eaten. Something small had been finding meals here, which made the room slightly more promising than a collection of bones.

I brought a few seeds beneath the lamp. They were shriveled, hard, and unfamiliar. I could not tell whether they were food that had gone bad, food that had always looked like that, or something people in this world fed exclusively to animals. The translation spell had not provided grocery training.

I put them back. There were things growing beneath the lower shelf that I did not want closer to my face.

While I was checking the last basket, a mouse emerged from a crack beside the door.

We both stopped, the mouse with its nose lifted and me with a basket lid in my hand. It was small, brown, and entirely without armor, and no window appeared beside it. Its only apparent qualification for living here was that it could fit through places I could not.

"Hello," I said quietly.

Its ears moved. It stayed beside the crack.

"I am having a difficult day."

That seemed to be enough information. It slipped beneath the shelf, found one of the fallen seeds, and carried it back toward the doorway. I lowered the basket lid and crouched, keeping a comfortable distance between my hands and the floor.

After pausing to examine a loose thread beside my wrapped foot, the mouse decided against it and went to the basin.

I watched the mouse drink. Its nose twitched between mouthfuls, and once it stopped to wash its face, too occupied to care about the stranger beside it. I found myself breathing more quietly so I would not frighten it.

The room held only the trickle and those small movements. I still glanced at the doorway whenever something clicked in the gutter. It took effort to believe I could stay here without waiting for the next attack.

I picked up a seed from the shelf and placed it beside the wall, reminding myself that carrying things and setting them down had been safe all morning. The mouse waited until my hand was well away before collecting it.

"You are welcome," I said.

It sat on its haunches to eat, turning the seed between its front paws. I smiled before I noticed that my hand had lifted from my knee. The fur between its ears looked soft.

Then I remembered the warmth of the wolf beneath my palm. I folded my hands in my lap and kept them there.

The mouse had come close enough to share the room. That could be enough.

I stayed until it finished eating and disappeared through a hole beside the gutter, with the ease of a creature that had already solved the problem of living here. The opening was narrower than my wrist. I was glad the mouse had somewhere to go.

Back beside the basin, I checked the knot over my forearm. A small ordinary creature was eating breakfast somewhere behind the wall. That was happening at the same time as whatever the monsters were doing. The place contained more than things waiting for me to enter sword range.

I thought of my mother calling to ask whether I was eating. Usually I told her yes while deciding what to buy on the way home. She would have questions when I failed to answer. So would my manager, though his would arrive with considerably less concern about my lunch.

With no signal, there was little reason to switch on the phone and watch the nearly empty battery in the corner of its screen. I kept it off, saving what remained for a photograph of a useful sign or a little light when the lamps stopped working.

*1. Find food. 2. Find a way out. 3. Stay alive long enough to need both.*

The order might require adjustment. At least I had a list.

As I rose, I watched the water arriving from somewhere above. People had built and maintained this channel, used the room beside it for stores, and carried loads between levels on the stairs and platforms. They had come here for reasons that did not involve fighting.

I wanted to find one of those routes before I damaged it.

The gloves helped with small mistakes. When a floor tile tipped beneath my foot, I caught myself against an iron bracket and felt the silver stitches warm. The bracket bent a little instead of tearing out of the wall. When I deliberately pushed a loose stone, it still shot across the room. I was dangerous. I simply had a little more room to make mistakes.

I took the sword out to practice where the corridor widened. A hard swing barely marked a rotten post. Moving the blade gently cut through it and scored a deep groove in the stone behind it. A pebble thrown normally bounced along the floor; one tossed with almost no effort buried itself in a distant slab.

I had seen versions of both experiments in the palace. Repeating them alone was less educational than I had hoped. Nobody could tell me whether I was doing it wrong, or doing it right, which was what frightened me.

What worried me most was how difficult it was to produce the same result twice. A tap that seemed gentle broke a tile. Another broke a whole step, though afterward I found an old crack running through its underside. The stone mattered. So did the difference between what I meant to do and what my cold, shaking fingers actually did. I had no useful way to measure either before something broke.

*Would a handshake destroy someone? Would a high-five remove the arm? Could I ever pet a cat again?*

I stopped with the sword half raised.

"No. That is unacceptable."

My voice sounded small in the empty passage, but the objection was sincere. A way home mattered. Food mattered. Not being stabbed by the dead mattered. I would still like to have something waiting on the other side of survival besides a lifetime of apologizing from across the room.

I put the sword away and closed my hands carefully. I would learn to control this cursed skill. I would escape the dungeon. I would find the people who had summoned me and make them finish the job properly.

And someday, I would pet a cat. Normally.

I left the splintered post and walked along the corridor again. The gutter continued beside the wall. I followed it beneath two more blue lamps, keeping clear of the loose stones I had used for practice. The floor sloped down toward a bend that hid the passage ahead.

A deep rumble reached me through the soles of my feet. I stopped before the bend and watched little ripples cross the gutter. Metal scraped against stone somewhere ahead. I brought my hand toward the sword, then waited, listening for a step that would tell me something was coming around the corner.

None followed. I still did not move until the water settled.

*Please let that be machinery.*

Once the sound faded, I crept along the wall and around the bend. The corridor ended at a doorway high enough to admit a wagon, blue light spilling across its threshold. I stopped short of it to listen again.

From there, I could see a chamber several times wider than the passage. Bones and broken weapons covered the floor. Blue lamps lit rows of empty nooks along the walls. Near the center of the room, a stone counterweight hung in an iron cradle, its chains rising into a square shaft in the ceiling. Rust flaked from the rails supporting it. One long brace ran from the cradle to a cracked pillar just inside the doorway, on my left.

Under it sat something the size of a small house.

Black scales covered its shoulders. Four arms rested on the floor around it, each beside a weapon too large for a person to lift. Two horns curved backward from its head. An old sword stood through its chest, the hilt rising between its collarbones. That should have been encouraging evidence that someone else had already dealt with the problem.

I stayed at the threshold, trying to see past the heap beneath the counterweight. The far wall was lost behind it. One step would give me a better angle, and the doorway would still be at my back.

My shoe touched the chamber floor.

Its golden eyes opened.

I stopped just inside the doorway, afraid even of shifting my feet. For a moment, neither of us moved. Then its gaze settled on me.

A red window replaced the blue one.

> **Grave Tyrant Malgorth**
>
> **Creature:** Gravebound Colossus.
> **Creature Type:** Undead.
> **Size:** Huge.
> **Hit Points:** 168 / 168.
> **Armor Class:** 18 (natural armor).
> **Condition:** Active.
> **Traits:** Multiattack.
>
> **Threat:** Lethal.
> **Recommendation:** RUN.

"Oh. Oh, n—"

Malgorth rose in a rattle of bones sliding from its lap. Its upper hands closed around a battle-axe and a broad sword, while the lower two collected a hooked blade and something that looked like an iron door attached to a handle.

"Oh no!"

Its first step shifted bones across the floor. The old sword moved with its chest, apparently more of an accessory than a wound. I backed toward the threshold, keeping my eyes on the nearest weapon.

A chain rattled above my head. I turned just as a stone slab dropped across the entrance, close enough for its rush of air to touch my face. It struck the floor with a crash, and the corridor vanished. I stood staring at the stone where the way out had been. The lamps brightened as though somebody wanted an excellent view of what happened next.

"Of course!"

I put my palm against the slab. The stone was cold and solid, and I wanted to push before I had even looked up at the chains. I made myself stop. If the doorway came down with it, I would have to get through falling stone.

A shadow crossed my hand.

I turned and stumbled sideways as the axe struck where my feet had been. Bones rolled beneath my shoe, dropping me onto my back. The broad sword passed above my face; I felt its air move my hair before it struck somewhere beyond me. Pieces of stone hit my back as I rolled onto my side and crawled for the cracked pillar.

The hooked blade dragged through the heap behind my legs. I pulled them in and squeezed into the space behind the pillar, beneath the brace joining it to the cradle.

A sting in my calf drew my attention to a tear in my pants, where blood was running into my remaining shoe. I could still put weight on the leg, which was the full medical examination I had time for.

Malgorth pulled the axe free and turned toward the pillar.

My sword's grip was slick inside my glove when I drew it. All I had to do was move it gently enough to make contact, just as I had practiced. Unfortunately, my shoulders were locked, my teeth were clenched, and every part of me wanted to hit the thing as hard as possible.

*Hard swings are useless. Fear makes me tense. Tensing makes me push harder.*

I looked at the four weapons gathering above the pillar.

*So the more terrified I become, the worse I fight. Excellent design.*

"You have got to be kidding me."

The hook scraped around the pillar. I tried to pull the cloak in after my knee, but the blade caught its edge and the collar snapped tight against my throat. My next breath stopped halfway in.

I bent backward, shoulder scraping stone, and got my fingers beneath the collar. A firm pull gave me enough room to breathe, though the fabric stayed taut. Every movement of the hook tugged me toward the pillar's edge.

I could let the cloak go. That would leave me with cold hands, a bleeding leg, and three useful items of which one had recently become a leash. I hated how long it took to make a decision that obvious.

The hook grated against the pillar. Its shaft was wood, bound with dark metal near the blade. I had cut a post with this sword a few minutes earlier. I looked at the shaft, then at my blade, and tried to remember the movement instead of the monster holding the other end.

I lowered the sword toward the shaft, stopping when the edge settled against wood. My right hand wanted to squeeze harder. I loosened my shoulder instead, keeping the blade in place while the collar pressed against my throat.

A small, deliberate push split the haft. The hooked end dropped beside my knee, and the cloak fell loose across my chest. I drew a breath that hurt, pulled the fabric free, and scrambled farther behind the pillar before the monster could bring another weapon around it.

*That worked. I actually meant to do that.*

For a moment, the relief was enormous. I had chosen a target, reached it, and produced something useful. There had been no accidental hole in a wall and no need to explain myself to anyone afterward.

Malgorth looked at the short piece of wood in its lower hand and let it fall.

Three weapons remained.

The broad sword scraped over the top of the pillar. I crouched until my nose almost touched my knee. Its edge knocked stone loose above me, and the pieces landed on my shoulders. The monster had no reason to let me finish congratulating myself.

An axe strike stopped me from getting around the other side, scattering fragments beneath my shoe. I almost fell with my weight half shifted. My calf had begun to throb, hurting when I stood on it and more when I tried to move quickly.

The entrance slab was only a few paces away. If I could get a hand against it and choose where to push, I might make an opening. I looked at the doorway while Malgorth worked the axe out of the floor.

Above the slab, a chain ran to a wheel fixed beneath the ceiling. A second length crossed toward the counterweight's frame. I could not tell what would come down if I broke the stone holding them. The platform upstairs had looked usable until a piece fell out of it. This mechanism was attached to something that could put an axe through the floor.

I would have to reach the slab before I could make any of those questions matter.

I put one foot toward the entrance while Malgorth worked the axe free. Its lower arm moved at once. The iron door swept through the space in front of me, close enough that I could see the raised rust around its dents.

I pulled back behind the pillar, scraping my injured calf against a broken step. The pain made me hold the leg still for a moment. The doorway was only a few paces away, but I could not find a way to take the first one.

*I cannot run around four arms. I can barely run around one piece of furniture.*

I had been thinking of the Skill as a way to make any weapon useful. It did nothing for the time it took me to get my feet underneath me. I needed cover, a reachable target, and a moment when the monster's weapons were somewhere else. So far, I had been getting those moments by falling down.

Malgorth set its feet wider. I watched the upper sword rise, then saw its lower hand turn the iron door toward the pillar. There was nowhere farther behind the stone to go.

I held my sword across my body and tried to make myself small.

The iron door came down. The pillar cracked beside my ear, and the brace sagged with a metallic groan. I lurched away as stone scraped my shoulder. My sword caught between two fallen pieces, jerking my hand to a stop.

I pulled once. The hilt moved; the blade did not. Malgorth stepped over the wreckage, lifting its axe. I could keep pulling or get my hand free.

I let go of the sword. My empty hand was shaking so badly that I could see the silver stitches move.

*Relax. Relax. Relax.*

A monster was about to turn me into paste. There was no reason for my body to find this instruction persuasive.

I shut my eyes for the length of a breath. At first, all I could hear was the metal shifting above me. I tried to put another sound beside it: rain against my apartment window, the quiet click of a cup set down after tea. The old blanket was over my knees, its thin corner tucked beneath my foot. The alarm was still several hours away.

I had to let the breath out before any of that felt real enough to help.

My shoulders loosened enough to let me uncurl my fingers, though bravery remained well out of reach.

The axe struck the rubble beside me. I tried to scramble backward, but a broken spear caught my wrapped foot and turned me sideways. When I looked up, Malgorth was bent over the fallen pillar, close enough that I could see grit lodged between its black scales.

Its nearest arm stretched over the broken pillar, leaving its stomach within reach. I ducked beneath it because moving away had stopped being possible, and brought my left hand toward its scales. My fingers were open. I concentrated on keeping them that way.

I rested my palm against its scales with the care of someone trying to comfort a crying child.

"There, there."

Malgorth looked down at me.

*Please be enough.*

I gave the smallest push I could manage, away from me.

Malgorth staggered backward three heavy steps, its weapons falling from its hands, and struck the hanging counterweight. The damaged brace snapped. Rusted rails tore out of cracked sockets, the cradle tipped, and the counterweight fell across the monster. Chains dragged through the overhead pulleys with a crash that seemed to go on far longer than one crash had any right to.

I threw myself beneath the pillar's remains as the chains screamed overhead. Through a gap between two stones, I saw a pulley mounting wrench free of the shaft wall. The masonry broke around it, and pieces of the cover struck the floor close enough to make me curl my knees toward my chest.

Rubble kept coming. I held my forearm over my head and looked through the gap whenever the dust thinned. Beyond the loose chains and the broken cover, a pale patch appeared.

Actual daylight.

For one beautiful second, I forgot everything except the fact that the sky still existed.

Then a chunk of stone the size of my head landed beside my knee.

"Oh, come on!"

With dust filling my mouth, I crawled farther beneath the broken pillar as the iron cradle settled with a crash nearby. Loose stones lodged across the shaft, narrowing the daylight to a gap too high to reach and too small to offer any immediate escape.

When the falling stopped, one horn and part of a scaled hand showed beneath the counterweight. Neither moved. The red window faded, and two blue ones crowded into its place.

> **Grave Tyrant Malgorth defeated.**
>
> **Hit Points:** 0 / 168.
> **Condition:** Dead.
>
> **Impact recorded:** Very low applied force.
> **Designation updated:** Gentle Calamity.
>
> Additional curse assessment available.

> **Name:** Haruto Minase.
>
> **Level:** 2.
> **Class:** None.
> **Hit Points:** 2 / 7.
> **Armor Class:** 12 (unarmored).
> **Spell Slots:** None.
>
> **Condition:** Injured, Fatigued.
> **Occupation:** None.
> **Designation:** Gentle Calamity.
> **Title:** Gentle Hands.

I read my new designation twice.

"Gentle Calamity?"

I looked at the buried monster, the broken cradle, and the shaft I had briefly mistaken for a rescue plan. My calf was bleeding. My sword was trapped somewhere under the debris. The new designation had left my level and armor exactly where they were, and my hit points had fallen to two. I had won the fight by being gentle, and all the damage was now my problem.

"Could we concentrate on the calamity part? I think that is the useful information."

Another window appeared.

> Reveal additional conditions?
>
> **YES / NO**

I crawled out from under the stones and sat against the wall. My stomach cramped with hunger. The new gloves were covered in gray dust, and my left hand was still shaking. I wanted an explanation. I wanted a weakness I could work around, a rule I could actually measure, or a sentence that began with 'To remove this curse.'

Before answering, I pulled the torn fabric clear of my calf. Blood had made it stick, and lifting it brought back the sting I had barely noticed during the fight. I folded the loose flap into a pad and held it over the cut, then took a strip from the cloth around my other ankle to keep it in place.

The knot would not come together. I watched my stiff right fingers miss the same loop twice and stopped when they cramped. After a breath, I tried again. This time it held.

The bleeding slowed, though the pain remained. I leaned my head against the wall, listening to the machinery settle.

Through the gap in the shaft, the daylight had a warmer color than the lamps. I could see a little cloud moving past it. Somewhere above me, wind was crossing a mountain. People were walking along roads, eating meals, and doing things that would not fit under **Catastrophic Pacifist**.

"Help," I called.

My voice traveled up the shaft. I kept my head lifted after it, waiting so hard that every little shift of stone sounded almost like an answer. Nothing came.

I tried again, louder, and the dust in my throat made me cough. A few grains fell from the broken cover. I watched them land on my knees.

The opening was high above the counterweight, with nothing I could climb safely between them. Even if I reached it, I would still have to get past the stones lodged across the shaft. For now, it was a view of somewhere I wanted to be.

I looked for my sword. Part of the hilt showed beneath chunks of stone piled against the broken cradle. I left it where it was. Pulling that arrangement apart could wait until I had stopped shaking.

The cloak had a small tear where the hook caught it. I brought its edges together over my knees and felt the ring's warmth return as the sweat cooled on my hand. I had survived. It seemed reasonable to spend a moment understanding that before I tried to do anything else.

Then I looked back at the waiting question. For once, the window was offering information instead of documenting my humiliation after the event.

*An explanation. That would be new.*

I stared at YES. The letters brightened under my gaze.

"How much worse could this possibly get?"

"Yes," I said.

> **Additional condition:** Affection Multiplier.

"Affection?"

The text explained on the window.

> **Affection Multiplier**
>
> The more sincere the bearer's emotional attachment to the target, the greater the resulting impact.
>
> - Applies when Inverse Impact activates.
> - Ordinary contact remains safe.
> - Maximum impact unchanged.

I sat very still.

I glanced at Malgorth. I had not become emotionally attached to it. "There, there" had been a desperate attempt to keep my shoulders loose. Apparently the window had saved this detail until after I had formed plans involving an animal I actually wanted to touch.

I thought of the mouse beside the basin and the impulse to rub a finger between its ears. I had been worried about shaking hands. I had been worried about using a sword. Before entering this room, I had made what seemed a reasonable decision: surviving long enough to pet a cat was a goal worth pursuing.

*A cat I actually like would be worse.*

I lowered my hand into my lap.

"No."

A final line appeared beneath the explanation.

> **Warning: Kissing is not recommended.**

For a moment, I could hear nothing except my own breathing.

Then I shouted, "NOOOOOOOOOOOOO!"

The word rose through the shaft and came back at me from the upper passages. Below the chamber, something shifted with a slow, heavy scrape. A chain hanging through a crack in the floor drew taut. I watched one link fall, then another.

Whatever was pulling it was much farther down than I wanted anything to be.

A low sound from beneath the floor stopped my shouting, growing until I could feel it through the wall at my back.

*Please be machinery.*

Something roared. It sounded awake.
