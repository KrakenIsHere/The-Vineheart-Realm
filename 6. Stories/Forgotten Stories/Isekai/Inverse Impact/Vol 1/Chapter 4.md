"It broke the pillar first," I said.

Nessa stood beside me on the landing, with the open gate behind us. Through the narrow windows on our left, I could see the reservoir. The wooden pipe ran along the wall on our right. Ahead, the stairs climbed around the corner where her lantern light had first appeared.

She looked from my face to the empty scabbard.

"Start before the pillar."

I started again.

There was a limit to how much dignity I could preserve while describing a fight I had spent mostly on the floor. I explained the closing door, the four arms, and the weapons. I told her how the hooked blade had caught my cloak, how the pillar had broken, and how I had lost the sword beneath it. When I reached the part where I had put my hand against the monster's stomach, she lowered the lantern a little.

"You got that close?"

"It got that close to me."

"And you pushed it into the weight."

"Yes. The frame was already damaged. The weight came down, then something broke higher up the shaft."

For a while, she said nothing. Water moved inside the pipe beside us. Beneath the windows, a chain rattled, stopped, and rattled again.

"That fits," she said at last.

I would have preferred an explanation that did not.

"I think I started the collapse."

"You said the pillar broke before you touched the guard."

"It did. But I sent it into the machinery."

She watched me while I said it. I kept my hands at my sides. There was no useful way to arrange them that made them look less involved.

"I needed to get away," I said. "I did not know you were down here."

"You know now."

"Yes."

She lifted the lantern toward the stairs.

"Then we work with what is still standing."

I followed her up the first flight. At the turn, she stopped beside a low arch leading left into a small room. The stairs continued upward on our right, toward a door with a white stripe painted across it.

"The service stair," she said, pointing to that door. "You can look from the threshold."

She opened it. Its lower edge dragged, and the hinges complained in much the same voice as every other hinge I had met. Beyond it, a short passage ended at a broken landing. The next flight continued upward on the far side of a gap wider than I was tall. Pale stone and torn iron showed along both edges. Far below, water caught the lantern light.

I remained beside the door.

The stairs on the other side were dry. They went up. I could see three of them clearly, and several more beyond the edge of the light.

*An exit can be real and still be completely useless to me.*

"Do people know you are here?" I asked.

"The crew knows which pipe I came to repair."

"Will they come looking?"

"They should. Whether they can reach this side is another question."

She pulled the door shut. I heard the latch settle before she turned back toward the low arch.

"The other steps join that flight higher up. We need the inspection ledge to reach them."

I looked once more at the white stripe on the door, then followed her into the room.

It was a control room, though considerably smaller than the words made me expect. A narrow window overlooked this end of the reservoir. Below it, a little work platform projected beside the outlet weight's iron frame. Five stone steps descended from our room to the platform. A closed hatch at the platform's far corner covered another stair going down toward the water.

Nessa hung the lantern on a hook inside the room. Through the window, I could see the stone weight moving between two upright rails. A sheet of iron covered the side facing the work platform. Its lower edge had folded inward, leaving a bright scrape where the moving cradle caught it.

The weight descended, struck the fold with a bang, and rose again.

The sound was much worse from here.

"That is the outlet?"

"The weight opens it. A chain goes over the pulley and down to the gate in the channel. When the weight comes down, the gate goes up."

She pointed through the window. I followed her finger from the cradle to the chain above it, then across the pulley toward a narrow shaft beside the reservoir wall.

"It cannot come down far enough," she said. "The gate barely opens. Water pushes it shut again, and the weight comes back up."

I watched one more repetition. A dark line on the wall marked a level the water had already passed. Another, higher line remained dry.

"And it has been doing that since yesterday?"

"With a few pauses when I thought I had persuaded it to improve."

On the platform below, a wrench and two wooden wedges rested well clear of the moving cradle. One wedge had a crushed end. I could see a hammer beside the hatch.

Nessa had been working while I found her food, ate some of it, and slept under her table.

I looked down at my bandaged leg.

"What do we need to do?"

"Stop the weight above the damage. Remove that bent cover. Then let it descend far enough to lift the gate."

"Can I help?"

She glanced at my hands.

"You can carry things."

It was a reasonable answer. I tried to hear it as one.

The room held a cupboard, a stool, and a board marked with lines I could not read. A coil of thick rope hung beside the cupboard. Nessa took a cloth bag from its bottom shelf and shook it open. There were rust stains around a hole near the seam.

"First, I want something to eat," she said.

"The workroom?"

"You made a route to it. Show me."

She took the lantern from its hook and lifted the empty bag.

---

I went back down the stairs and stopped at the service gate. The iron bar still rested on the floor beyond it, where she had asked me to leave it.

"May I take that?"

Nessa looked at it, then at me.

"Carry it with the wedge pointed down."

I picked it up by the middle. She closed the gate behind us, lifted it slightly with the wrench so the latch would settle, and left the key in the lock on our side.

"So we can get back in," she said.

I had been about to ask.

We followed the pipe out through the covered passage and down the four steps. The reservoir was on our right now. I kept the wall on my left. At the opening beneath the arch, I pointed out the bent rail and the empty socket where its fastening had been.

Nessa stopped well short of it.

"You pushed the stone from that side?"

"Toward the water."

She examined the arch, then crouched to look through the hole. A few pieces of mortar came away beneath her fingers. I watched her brush them aside.

For a moment, I was jealous of someone cleaning a doorway.

"The arch is holding," she said. "Keep your head low."

She went through first, carrying the lantern close to the floor. I followed with the bar in front of me, taking care to lift it over the loose pieces. My pad caught the edge of a stone. I stopped, moved my foot clear, and continued. There was no prize for doing this quickly.

On the other side, I showed her the charcoal arrow beside the steps. From there, I led her through the second round room, back over the little channel bridge, and through the racks. At the first round room, my three pebbles still marked the passage toward the sorting-room landing.

Nessa paused beneath the signpost.

"Do you want to know what these say?"

"Very much."

She pointed to each board in turn. One named the sorting gallery. One named an upper channel. The last led to a drain passage.

I copied short Japanese labels onto the slate with firm charcoal strokes. Then I added a little arrow beside the route we had just used. None of the boards said anything about leaving.

"Does this place have an exit sign?"

"Several."

"I would enjoy meeting one."

At the broken crossing, she held the lantern toward the fallen iron. She examined the pale sockets, the torn supports, and the carts beneath them. Her mouth tightened.

"I crossed that yesterday."

The platform did not move while we watched. A hanging chain tapped one cart. I had heard the sound before. Hearing it beside someone who remembered the walkway whole was different.

We left the broken crossing behind and went through the sorting room. The skeleton still drew its scraper along the trough. Nessa walked down the aisle without looking toward it until she reached the door at the stairs.

I kept pace with her, watching the wooden handle rise and fall beyond the rail.

"Does it ever stop?" I whispered.

"Not while I have been here."

"Does anyone ask it to?"

"I repair the water gates."

That seemed to be the limit of the services available.

At the bottom of the stairs, I recognized my triangle of pebbles. We followed the gutter to the basin, and I stopped across from the open workroom.

Nothing had changed. The bar stood beside the door. The bench was where I had left it. My coin still waited on the table beside the bundle.

Nessa went straight to the food.

I had never been so relieved to watch someone untie a piece of cloth.

She set the lantern on the table, took bread and cheese, filled the flask at the basin, and came back to sit on the bench. I remained near the table, holding the bar until she pointed to an empty patch of floor.

"You can put that down."

I did.

She cut the cheese with a little knife from her apron and passed me a piece of bread with some on top. I looked at the food, then at the coin.

"That was my last silver."

"I will account for it when we get back."

"Does it cover the pads?"

"The pads are borrowed. So is the tool."

"I would like to return them."

"That is usually how borrowing works."

I sat at the other end of the bench. The cheese was hard, salty, and considerably more welcome than its appearance suggested. I ate slowly. Across the corridor, the basin splashed, stopped, and began trickling again.

Nessa listened to it while she chewed.

"Has it been doing that all night?"

"Yes."

"Then the outlet is still catching."

She looked tired enough that I wanted to suggest sleeping under the table. The water answered that idea with another spurt over the rim.

"Should we hurry?"

"We should eat. Then we should get the weight secured before we put anything near it."

I finished the piece she had given me. When she offered another, I accepted without discussing the finances again.

For a few minutes, the most important thing I did was chew.

*A chair would improve this considerably. I am willing to start with bread.*

Nessa picked up the coin and put it inside a small pocket in her apron. Then she began loading the bag. The remaining food went in first, followed by the flask, a tin of grease, and several wooden wedges from the shelf. She checked each wedge for splits before choosing it.

I unwrapped the slate from my cloak.

"Could this go in there too?"

She held the bag open. I put the slate, the old gloves with the cord and charcoal, and the spare pad beside the bundle. With my belt relieved of several responsibilities, I put the cloak back around my shoulders.

Nessa glanced at its silver stitching, then at the gloves I was wearing.

"Where did those come from?"

"A chest fell out of a shelf after I broke three skeletons."

She waited.

I considered whether there was a version of that sentence worth trying again.

"The gloves help when I lose my balance," I said. "A little. They do not stop me breaking things on purpose."

"Good to know."

"They are good gloves."

"I believe you."

It occurred to me that I was defending the most reassuring gift I had found in a room full of body parts. Nessa tied the bag closed before I could make that sound any better.

At the doorway, she stopped.

"Where were you trying to go before you fell?"

"A survey lodge. On the southern road. Past a small bridge and three white stakes."

"The survey road is above the old workings. You came down through the forest?"

"Through the forest, then through the floor."

"Did someone send you to the lodge?"

"The palace."

She looked back at me.

The man who had robbed me had advised against mentioning that part. I had reached the point where keeping it secret would require lying to someone who had shared her bread and knew the stairs.

"I was summoned," I said. "Apparently they wanted a champion."

"You?"

I looked down at my repaired foot, then at the little bar on the floor.

"There were some problems with the result."

Nessa rested the bag against her hip.

"How long ago?"

"A few days. I came from somewhere very far away."

I did not know how to explain a train to someone standing beside a brazier. I could explain losing a shoe. We had started with something that translated well.

"They could not keep me near the terraces after the tests," I said. "They told me to stay at the lodge while they looked for a way to send me home."

"They sent you alone?"

"Ordan took me to the watchpost. Then something needed repairing, and he went back. I was supposed to follow the main road."

"What happened?"

I told her about the men, the bag, and the slimes. I kept it short. There were only so many occasions on which I could explain that I had lost everything without beginning to sound like a person who misplaced things professionally.

Nessa looked toward the corridor when I finished.

"Did the keeper ever see you?"

"I never reached the lodge."

"Then whoever goes there to look for you will find an empty room."

I had thought about the keeper. I had thought less about someone returning to the palace with an answer.

"The princess wanted someone to come after me," I said. "They did not promise when."

Nessa adjusted the bag's strap over her shoulder.

"We get out first. Then you can tell someone which room to look in."

It was the first plan involving the palace that began with something I could understand.

Nessa took the lantern from the table.

I picked up the bar.

---

When we reached the control room again, the water had climbed closer to the upper stain on the wall. The weight still struck the bent cover on every descent. Nessa put the bag on the cupboard and took out the wedges.

"Stand here," she said, placing the lantern on the windowsill. "Tell me when the cradle comes back up."

She went down the five steps to the work platform. I stayed in the room, with the window in front of me. The weight was below and a little to my right. Nessa stood on its left, outside the two rails. Between her and the moving cradle was a waist-high iron guard.

She lifted a long handle fixed beside the frame. A catch moved toward the teeth of a wheel above her. The chain rattled through another short movement.

"Coming up," I said.

"Keep watching."

The cradle rose above the bent part of the cover. I could see clear space beneath its lower edge.

"Above it."

Nessa pulled the handle down. The catch entered the toothed wheel with a clack. The chain tightened. The weight stopped.

For the first time since reaching the reservoir, I heard water without the bang that usually interrupted it.

Nessa waited, looking from the chain to the catch. Then she fitted a thick pin through a second hole beside the handle. It stopped short. She withdrew it, moved the handle a fraction, and tried again. This time the pin went through.

"Does that hold it?"

"The catch holds the wheel. The pin keeps the handle from moving."

She pointed to both. I nodded once I could tell them apart.

"Come down. Stay on the steps until I tell you where to stand."

I took the lantern from the sill and followed. The platform had room for two people beside the guard, provided neither decided to be somewhere else suddenly. The hatch was behind Nessa, near the wall. Below the guard, I could see the lower part of the iron cover bent toward the cradle's path.

She placed the wedges beneath a ledge on the cradle, using the head of her wrench to slide them into the gap from the side. Her hands stayed outside the frame.

"If the catch slips, those should stop it before the bend," she said. "We still keep out from underneath."

I looked up at the chain. Several links were polished where they crossed the pulley. Beyond that, rust darkened them again.

I had watched a chain tear stone out of a ceiling. It had given me enough information about the arrangement of my body beneath heavy objects.

Nessa crouched beside the damaged cover. Its bottom edge had caught against the side of the cradle. Four bolts fixed it to stone sockets outside the rails. The cover did not carry the weight. It prevented people from putting hands into the guide while it moved.

The purpose seemed clear enough. I regretted having a reason to learn it.

She tried the lowest bolt with the wrench. Her shoulders tightened. The wrench moved a little, slipped, and struck the platform.

I drew back before she tried again.

This time she changed the angle. The bolt remained where it was. Her knuckles came away with a line of rust across them.

"That one was already bad," she said. "The shift has pulled it sideways."

I held the lantern closer while she examined the socket. Then she tested the bolt above it. That one moved. Slowly, with a rough scrape at each turn, it came free. She set it in a hollow beside the hatch, removed the other upper bolt, and worked the cover's upper edge loose.

The sheet of iron shifted toward her, but its bottom remained fixed.

"Could we leave it like that?" I asked.

"It would catch the cradle again."

I looked at the bar in my hand. Its wedge was narrower than the gap she had opened.

"Could I try this? With firm pressure."

She studied the bar, then pointed to a space at the top of the fold.

"Here. Away from the rail. Keep the end against the cover."

I set down the lantern and fitted the wedge into the gap. Nessa remained beside the steps, where she could see both my hand and the catch above the weight.

I pushed firmly. The cover creaked. I moved it a little farther from the cradle, then stopped when the bar began sliding sideways.

The lower bolts did not move.

I tried once more with my feet set wider. My right calf tightened. The bar pressed a small dent into the iron. Nothing came loose.

There was no hidden supply of ordinary strength beneath my curse. I had become very powerful at the part of the job involving making the wrong amount of effort.

"Enough," Nessa said. "You will slip."

I lifted the bar clear and stepped back onto the lowest stair.

The weight remained still. The water continued rising.

"We can chisel the heads off," she said, looking at the two bolts still holding the fold. "It will take time."

I could break timber. I could break stone. I had also broken enough things around both to understand why she was reaching for the chisel.

"I might be able to break that part," I said.

Nessa did not immediately answer.

This was an improvement over being asked to do it before anyone considered the room.

"How much would break?" she asked.

"I do not know. I can choose where I push. I cannot promise how far something will go."

She put the chisel down, picked up the lantern, and examined the cover again. This time she checked the stone around the sockets, then the rails behind it. The light stayed well outside the frame.

"If the sheet comes off, those rails still hold the cradle," she said. "If a rail goes with it, we leave the catch in."

"Could it come down anyway?"

"Yes."

I looked at the wedges beneath the weight. They seemed considerably smaller now that I knew what we were asking them to do.

Nessa stood and pointed toward the platform's empty back wall. It was behind the hatch, several feet to the left of the bent cover.

"Can you make it go that way?"

I followed the direction with my eyes. The two bolts held the fold near its bottom. From beside the steps, I could put the wedge against the fold's right-hand edge and push sideways, along the platform. The cover would have to tear loose to move.

I had seen stone do that. I would have liked to have seen iron do it somewhere more forgiving first.

"I can push that way," I said. "The rest is what worries me."

"It worries me too."

She took the lantern up the steps and hung it inside the control room. Then she came back for the loose bolts and the chisel. I waited while she carried them out of the space where the cover might land.

"Only when I am up there," she said.

"Yes."

"And if you change where you want to push, tell me."

I nodded.

*This is a much better instruction than gently.*

She went up to the window. I remained beside the lowest stair, with the moving weight secured behind the guard on my right. The blank wall was to my left. I put the bar's wedge against the side of the fold, clear of the cradle and its chain.

My calf tightened when I bent my knees. I stopped and set my remaining shoe flat on the platform. The leather pad under my other foot shifted. I adjusted it before returning the wedge to the iron.

"Ready?" I asked.

"I am clear."

I kept my grip firm. The wedge rested against the iron without moving it. For a moment, I could hear water through the outlet shaft and the faint creak of a chain carrying more weight than I wanted to consider.

I had an enormous urge to brace myself and shove.

That would produce another dent, if I was fortunate. The movement that might help was the one I had spent yesterday failing to make while something swung a sword at me.

*There is time. She is clear. The weight is stopped.*

I let my shoulders loosen and gave the bar a light push toward the empty wall.

The cover tore sideways with a sound like a pan being folded in half.

One bolt head struck the wall before I saw it leave its socket. The other pulled free with a fistful of pale stone. The sheet hit the wall, bounced onto the hatch, and stopped with one corner folded underneath it.

I drew the bar back and took a step away. The leather pad turned beneath my left heel. My foot slipped off its edge, and I went down hard on my right knee.

Pain flashed up my leg. I made a sound I hoped the water had covered.

Above me, Nessa called my name.

"Here," I said. "I am here."

I remained on my knee, keeping my left hand against my thigh. Loose grit rattled across the platform. The weight had moved against the catch when the cover broke, but it had stopped again. I could still see both rails standing behind the guard.

Nessa waited until the last little piece of stone fell, then brought the lantern down the steps. She set it beside me on the lowest stair.

"Stay where you are."

"I had intended to."

She crouched beside me and looked at my leg. The bandage on my calf was dry. My knee was already developing an opinion of its own.

"Can you move your foot?"

I moved it. Then I straightened the leg a little, stopping before it began to shake.

"That hurts."

"You landed on stone."

It was comforting to have the cause confirmed by someone else.

The blue writing appeared when I looked for it.

> **Hit Points:** 6 / 7.
> **Condition:** Bruised, Sore.

One point gone to a shoe made out of someone else's knee protection. The curse had broken the iron. It had done nothing useful about my feet.

I set the bar beside the steps. Nessa helped me sit on the lowest one without asking me to push against anything. Her hand rested beneath my elbow while I moved my good foot. Nothing happened to her.

I had known ordinary contact was safe. It was still a relief to have an ordinary thing happen with someone else involved.

She untied the cord around the leather pad, turned it back beneath my foot, and threaded the heel loop through a different buckle.

"You need this across the back," she said. "Otherwise it folds when you step away."

I watched her hands. She pulled the knot tight, pressed the strap flat, and checked the pad's position.

"Better?"

I moved my foot without lifting it from the floor. The pad moved with it.

"Much."

"Tell me if it starts slipping again."

She stood, then gave the weight considerably more attention than she had given my knee. She checked the catch and pin, the chain, and both rails. At the socket where the stone had come away, she ran the end of the chisel along a new shallow crack.

I waited for her to tell me what else I had broken.

"The cover is off," she said. "The guide is still straight."

"Enough to use?"

"Enough to try. From upstairs."

I looked at the twisted sheet on the hatch. I had pictured it coming away like a loose board. It had arrived at the wall as something I would have difficulty recognizing if I had not watched it happen.

"That is more damage than I wanted."

"Yes."

She did not hurry to make the answer kinder.

"The outlet could not open with it there," she added. "Now it might."

I stood carefully and collected the bar. My knee hurt, but it took my weight. Nessa followed me up the five steps with the lantern and hung it inside the control room. She brought the handle's pin with her only after she had checked that the catch remained engaged.

The handle reached nearly to the room's threshold. Beside it, a small geared wheel connected to the mechanism below. From there she could work both without standing beside the cradle.

"Watch the gate shaft," she said. "If the chain stops while the weight keeps moving, tell me."

"What should it do?"

"Move together."

I found the two lengths of chain again, one descending to the weight and the other disappearing toward the outlet gate. Nessa withdrew the loose wedges with a long hooked rod from the cupboard. Then she turned the little geared wheel until the weight rose a fraction and the catch no longer carried the load. She raised the catch clear and let the wheel turn back beneath her hands.

The weight descended.

This time it passed the bright scrape where it had been stopping. The chain rolled over the pulley, and a deep wooden groan came from the outlet shaft.

Then water struck something below us with a sound that filled the room.

I flinched. Nessa held the handle steady.

"Moving?"

"Both chains. Yes."

The weight slowed near the bottom of its guide. She lowered the handle. The catch settled into another tooth, and the chain stopped. Water continued rushing through the open gate.

For several breaths, we looked at the reservoir without speaking.

The leaves gathered beneath the nearest lamp began to travel toward the outlet. The little circle they had been making lengthened, pulled apart, and disappeared down the side channel. A pale strip emerged beneath the old water stain on the wall.

Nessa put the pin through the handle's new position.

"It is draining," she said.

I watched until I could see the strip widen.

"I can see it."

She glanced at me, then back at the water.

I had done something useful. It had needed a stopped weight, a cleared platform, and someone who knew which part could be broken. My knee hurt. The iron cover would not be going back into place.

The water was going down.

For once, I could tell which part of the result I wanted to keep.

---

We waited in the control room while the water fell. Nessa kept checking the outlet chain. I sat on the stool with my right leg straight and watched the dark stain on the wall widen above the surface.

Below us, the tops of the inspection steps appeared first. Then I could see the ledge itself, pale beneath a sheet of retreating water.

It followed the end wall below us toward the opposite bank. Pillars stood along its outer edge, their bases joined by the narrow strip of stone. Near the far bank, the ledge turned toward a small stair below the door with the white rag on its handle.

Nessa pointed across the water.

"Those steps come up beneath that door. From there, the gallery takes us to the service stair above the break."

I followed the route with my eyes. Down from our platform. Along the end wall. Around the corner. Up on the other side.

For the first time, I could see enough of a possible exit to understand how my feet might reach it.

The surface continued dropping. Water streamed off the ledge and gathered into little channels between the pillar bases. I could see a darker patch halfway along it, where sediment had collected against a raised seam.

"That looks slippery."

"It will be. We wait until we can see where the water is moving."

She took the coil of rope from beside the cupboard and checked it a section at a time. I watched her bend one fuzzy section over her thumb, check the fibers beneath it, and return the coil to the floor.

"Could we go back to the workroom instead?" I asked.

"We could. The water is still entering through the smaller channels. This gives us time while the outlet stays open."

I looked toward the weight.

"And if it closes?"

"We want to be up the other steps before the ledge floods again."

The answer made staying on the stool considerably less attractive.

She checked the chain and the pin once more, then lifted the bag. I stood carefully. My knee had stiffened while I sat, but I could bend it, and the leather pad stayed beneath my left foot when I stepped backward.

Nessa saw me testing it.

"Still holding?"

"Yes."

"Then put the bar on the bag. You will want a hand free."

She made a loop with a short length of spare cord and secured the bar along the bag's outside, wedge down. I held the bag still while she worked. Carrying something had never seemed especially useful before this place. Now it gave me a job I could do without checking the far wall first.

She slung the bag over her shoulder, took the lantern, and led me down to the platform.

The torn cover still rested across the hatch. Nessa got her wrench beneath its loose corner and tipped it against the empty back wall. The iron scraped over stone, then settled with a heavy clang. I remained on the steps until she had moved clear.

She lifted the hatch. A rusty hinge squealed, and cool air reached us from below.

The stair descended along the end wall, with the reservoir beyond its left edge. Water dripped from the lowest treads. A heavy iron ring was fixed beside the opening. Nessa tied the rope to it, then lowered the rest of the coil onto a dry step.

"The brackets along the ledge take a line," she said. "We use one when we inspect the lower gates. I will put it through as we go."

I looked at the ring, then at the drop beyond the stairs.

"Do you do this often?"

"Usually with less water moving."

She went first. I followed several steps behind, keeping my remaining shoe flat on each tread before bringing the leather pad down beside it. The water sounded closer with every step. Beneath the outlet shaft, a current pulled foam and leaves away from the wall.

Nessa stopped at the bottom and held the lantern low.

The first stretch of ledge was clear. It was wide enough to walk along facing forward, provided I did not decide to let anyone pass. The wall would stay on my right. The reservoir would stay on my left. Water still ran over the stone near the next pillar, but I could see the edge beneath it.

"Wait there."

She walked to the pillar and secured the first stretch of rope to an iron bracket at her waist. Then she gathered the spare length and turned back.

"Now. Keep to the wall. Use the line for balance, but watch your feet."

I closed my left hand firmly around the rope. It had a rough, wet surface. I kept the pressure steady as I walked, moving one hand at a time along it. I wanted something to hold if my foot slipped. I did not want to discover what a frightened little tug would do to a bracket.

At the pillar, I stopped beside her. The outlet's rush made conversation difficult. She pointed to a strip of cleaner stone along the wall and waited while I placed my foot on it.

We went on that way, one short stretch at a time. She secured the line at each bracket. I followed once she had checked the next piece of ledge.

The world had become a wall, a line, and the next place to put a foot. I could manage that much.

At the darker patch I had seen from above, Nessa used the flat end of the wrench to scrape sediment away from the raised seam. Water carried the loosened dirt over the edge. She checked both sides with the lantern, then crossed.

I lifted my left foot over the seam instead of dragging the pad across it. Nothing caught. I stood still on the other side for a moment and let myself be pleased.

Then something struck iron inside the wall on my right.

Nessa stopped.

A recessed doorway opened a few paces ahead. Bars filled its frame. Beyond them, a short vestibule led to another barred gate farther inside. Water dripped from the ceiling between the two barriers.

The second impact came from beyond the inner gate.

I knew that low cry. I had heard it beneath Malgorth's chamber, then across the reservoir. From here, it sounded like something straining to finish a breath it no longer needed.

Nessa turned the lantern toward the recess.

A shape moved behind the inner bars.

It had two arms. That was the first thing I checked. They belonged to a body much larger than mine, with a broad belly beneath a torn leather covering. Gray skin hung loose along one shoulder. One side of its mouth had collapsed, leaving teeth visible where the lip should have been.

It took a step toward the gate. A chain dragged from an iron shackle around its ankle and disappeared into the dark behind it.

The red writing appeared beside its head.

> **Ogre Zombie**
>
> **Creature:** Ogre Zombie (reanimated ogre).
> **Creature Type:** Undead.
> **Size:** Large.
> **Hit Points:** 85 / 85.
> **Armor Class:** 8 (unarmored).
> **Condition:** Active, Chained.
>
> **Threat:** Lethal within reach.
> **Recommendation:** Keep the gates between you and the creature.

Eighty-five. I had been very pleased to get seven back.

The ogre reached the inner gate and put one hand around a bar. Its other arm hung at its side for a moment. Then it struck the iron with the heel of its palm.

The sound came through the ledge beneath my feet.

I stopped looking at the window and looked at the gate's frame. The mortar above it had a pale crack. I could not tell whether the crack had been there yesterday. That was becoming an unpleasantly important distinction.

"An ogre zombie," I said.

Nessa had already lowered the lantern. She watched its hand through the two sets of bars.

"Keep moving."

"The frame is cracked."

"I see it. We are outside the first gate. It is behind the second. We stay here."

I had put my hand against every serious problem since arriving. Several of them had become different problems immediately afterward.

The bar was tied to the bag. The dagger was wrapped inside my pocket. I could reach neither without stopping in front of something I very much wanted to leave behind.

I followed Nessa.

As we passed the recess, the ogre shifted its face along the bars to keep us in sight. The chain tightened behind it. A long scrape followed, somewhere inside the lower passage.

My hands wanted to close harder around the rope. I kept my grip firm and concentrated on the cleaner strip beside the wall.

One step. Bring the other foot forward. Leave room behind Nessa.

The ogre struck the gate again.

Dust sifted into the vestibule. Neither gate opened.

I continued until the curve around the next pillar hid the recess. Only then did I look back.

Nessa was waiting at the corner where the end wall met the far bank. She held the lantern toward the next section of ledge.

"You all right?"

"I would like it to remain where it is."

"So would I."

She fed the rope through the corner bracket.

There had been no fight. The writing remained red behind me until the pillar hid it. No new message congratulated me for walking away.

I had two people to get out now. I could count that without a window.

---

We turned left along the far bank. The wall remained on my right, the water on my left. Ahead, the little stair climbed toward the open doorway with the rag. Between us and its first tread, water still crossed the ledge in a narrow sheet.

Nessa stopped before it.

A shallow drain cut through the stone at the foot of the stairs. Its metal cover had shifted, leaving a dark gap along one side. Water poured through the gap toward the reservoir. The ledge itself continued beneath it. I could see enough stone on the wall side to place one foot, but the cover tilted whenever a stronger pulse came through.

"I do not trust that," I said.

"Neither do I."

She set the lantern on a bracket above the drain and took the bar from the bag. Its wedge slid beneath the raised corner of the metal cover. She pressed down, shifted her feet, and guided the cover back into its recess.

The metal scraped, settled, and stopped moving beneath the water. Nessa tested it with her boot before lifting the lantern again.

I waited while she did all of it.

My contribution was being available if something needed to become several feet less attached to the floor. This particular repair seemed better suited to someone who could push until it fit.

She put the bar back into its loop on the bag.

"Step where I step."

She crossed the drain close to the wall and reached the first stair. I followed, setting my good foot where hers had been. Cold water reached the cloth around my left toes before I lifted them clear.

The ring's warmth remained faint in my hand. It did very little about a wet sock.

At the first dry step, I looked down at the makeshift sole. The heel cord held. The pad was still beneath my foot.

*You may be ugly. I have become extremely fond of your continued presence.*

I kept walking before fondness could persuade me to pat it.

The stair climbed along the wall beneath the far gallery. Halfway up, it turned sharply right, taking us away from the water. I paused at the turn while Nessa looked through the open doorway above.

The bucket still stood outside it. The rag moved against the handle. Beyond was a straight passage, dry except for old stains near the floor. A blue lamp shone at its far end.

Nessa went in first. She set the bag down just inside the door, then held the lantern back toward me.

"Almost there."

I reached the last tread, stepped across the threshold, and stopped on dry stone.

For several breaths, I could only listen. The outlet was still loud, but it was below us now. The ogre's blows had become dull sounds through more than one wall.

Nessa looked back down the stair, then across the reservoir toward the weight we had left secured.

"It is still open."

"Can we leave it?"

"The crew will need to check the guide and replace the cover. The open outlet gives them room to work."

She pulled the door shut. Wood scraped over the threshold. The latch caught. She tried the handle once, then took her bag and continued along the passage.

I followed her past a row of narrow windows. Below them, the reservoir stretched toward the landing where I had first seen her lantern. The service gate was a little shape beneath a blue lamp. I could pick out my damaged railing farther along the near bank.

We had followed the end wall from the near bank and come up on the opposite side. My map would need another large correction. I was beginning to understand why people made maps before becoming lost.

The passage turned right at the end of the reservoir. After the turn, a broad flight climbed toward a closed wooden door. White paint marked the stone beside it.

Nessa stopped at the foot of the flight.

"The service stair," she said. "We are above the broken landing."

I looked up. The steps continued all the way to the door. There was no gap, no slab moving behind me, and no sword protruding from anyone within sight.

"That is a very good staircase."

Nessa looked at it, then at me.

"It has had a quiet day."

I climbed slowly. My right knee hurt on the higher steps. I used my good leg first, bringing the other up once I had somewhere firm to stand. Nessa waited whenever she reached the edge of the lantern's light.

Near the top, a draft reached the sweat at my neck.

I stopped.

It smelled of wet wood and pine.

There had been leaves in the water. There had been cold air in several passages. This air moved under a door at the top of a staircase someone had built to use.

I could hear another sound beyond it.

A voice.

Nessa set the bag on the landing and knocked with the metal ring. Three hard knocks, then two. The sound traveled through the wood and came back from somewhere larger beyond it.

"Nessa?"

She closed her eyes for a moment.

"Yes."

Feet moved on the other side. A bolt scraped. The door opened toward us, and she moved the bag out of its way.

Daylight reached the landing.

I lifted my hand to shield my eyes. Beyond the doorway was a covered work yard, with rain-dark boards under a stack of split wood. Farther out, pine branches moved beneath a gray sky.

Actual sky. Enough of it to stand under.

A man in a heavy coat stood beside the door with a lamp he no longer needed. He looked at Nessa, then at me. His eyes stopped at my empty scabbard and continued down to the leather pad under my foot.

"Who is that?"

I lowered my hand. My introduction had reached the point where several parts needed deciding before I opened my mouth. Summoned champion. Civilian. The person responsible for some of the repairs they were about to need.

Nessa picked up the bag.

"Haruto. He is with me."

The man stepped aside.

I went through the doorway after her, keeping my hands clear of the frame.

Cold air reached my face. I stood beneath the covered roof and looked past its edge at the trees. There would be questions. There was still a lodge, a palace, and a very large problem attached to my hands.

For now, I could see a road from where I stood.

Nessa glanced back.

"Coming?"

"Yes."

I followed her.
