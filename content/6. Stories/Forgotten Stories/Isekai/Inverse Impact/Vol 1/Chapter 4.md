Leaving the basin workroom, I followed the gutter back toward the junction with my pebbles. The basin and, farther beyond it, Malgorth's chamber were behind me. I kept the gutter on my left until the passage rose and the water disappeared through a hole beneath the wall.

I recognized the triangle before I reached it, the fourth stone still across its upper side. That small success improved my opinion of the entire morning.

Instead of trying the upper route to the broken lift again, I examined a dark recess opposite the two passages I knew. What I had mistaken for another empty nook opened onto a narrow staircase, hidden from the blue lamps by a turn in the wall.

A scuff in the dust on the lowest step made me kneel, keeping my hands on my knees. Someone's heel had disturbed the pale layer, leaving a darker oval patterned with ridges. My remaining shoe had ridges too, but I had never stood on this step.

I added the staircase to my slate with a broken line because I did not know where it went. Then I placed two pebbles at its mouth, one behind the other, pointing back into the junction.

The stairs climbed to the right for fourteen steps before turning left around a square pillar. At the top, I stopped at a closed wooden door with an iron ring for a handle and listened to a faint scrape followed by a hollow clack. The same sounds repeated while I waited.

After setting the iron bar on the step beside me, I lifted the ring with a clink against its fastening. Firm pressure opened the door just far enough for me to see through the gap, accompanied by a low creak from its hinges.

Beyond it was a long room divided down the middle by a rail. On the left, where I stood, an aisle ran between workbenches and the wall. On the right, a stone trough rested inside an iron frame. A chain passed beneath the frame and through a low opening at the far end of the room.

A skeleton stood beside the trough, green light in its sockets just like the soldiers. I stopped breathing for a moment before noticing the strip of leather over its shoulder and the long wooden scraper in its hands.

The skeleton dragged its scraper along the trough until it clacked against a stop, then lifted it back to the near end and began again.

There was nothing in the trough.

I watched three repetitions from behind the door. The skeleton did not turn. Dust covered its shoulders and collected inside its rib cage. Whatever it was doing, it had been doing it long enough to wear a pale line in the stone.

*No sword. That is a promising difference.*

I considered saying hello. Then I remembered how the last several skeletons had responded to my attempts at conversation.

From the doorway, I watched it finish another pass. A second door waited at the far end of the aisle, on my side of the rail, and I could reach it without approaching the skeleton. I picked up the bar and stepped inside.

When the scraper came toward me along the trough, I stopped beside the first workbench. The skeleton kept its head tilted toward the stone as the scraper clacked against the stop. I moved past the bench while it lifted the tool to begin again.

Shallow trays on the next bench held pieces of colored rock arranged by size, mostly pale with a few black pieces glossy beneath the dust. Small metal gauges rested beside them, but there was no coin, glowing sword, or helpful bread. People had worked here, sorting loads before sending them somewhere else.

By the time I reached the far door, the skeleton was making another pass, showing no interest in following me or stopping its work.

"Sorry about the interruption," I whispered.

The scraper clacked.

I held the far door's handle firmly and pushed. Its lower edge dragged over a raised corner of the floor with a harsh scrape. The skeleton continued its work behind me. I left before politeness became an experiment.

The door opened onto a landing above a chamber, where a gap in the iron railing marked the start of a broken walkway. Its far half hung lower, with supports pulled from the wall and pale fractures around the empty sockets. Beneath it, fallen iron had scraped bright lines through the rust on dusty carts. A swinging chain struck one cart with a hollow clang.

The fresh damage brought back the crash that had opened the sky above Malgorth. I looked at the door I had just dragged over the floor.

On the far side of the chamber, steps climbed toward a row of lamps. There was no way to reach them from where I stood. My side of the landing continued left to a short corridor and right to a smaller door. I nearly leaned on the railing to look down, then stepped back. A stumble, a little push to catch myself, and I could be following the walkway into the carts below.

I drew the broken crossing on the slate, then tried the door on the right.

A click from the latch and a dry squeal from the hinges accompanied my firm push on the door. Inside the closet, shelves held pots of dried pigment and a stack of wooden signs: a black hand raised beneath a red line, two people beside a wheel like the board near the broken lift, and several with writing but no pictures at all.

I held the hand sign toward the light. It might mean stop. It might mean gloves required. It might be an advertisement for a profession I no longer wanted associated with my title.

I put it back.

From the landing, I followed the corridor to the left. It sloped upward, passed beneath a low arch, and opened into a round chamber with three other exits. A blue lamp hung in the center. Beneath it stood a post with signs pointing in different directions.

Every sign had writing. I went around the post, checking the boards for pictures, then checked their backs in case someone had put the useful side out of sight.

None did.

"An impressive amount of writing for very little help," I said.

I put three pebbles beside the doorway I had entered, with a gap pointing back toward it. The way to the right had stairs going down. The opening ahead led uphill. The one on the left was level, with cooler air moving out of it.

For once, I chose the air instead of the stairs.

The passage bent twice before reaching a low room lined with empty racks. There were two doors on the far wall. The left stood open onto steps descending into darkness. Through the closed door on the right, I could hear water. I drew both on the slate, lifted the right door's latch, and pushed firmly. The wood groaned around its hinges. I waited until it stopped, then followed the sound.

The gently climbing passage brought me to a narrow bridge over a channel flowing toward the room I had left. Halfway across, I stopped to watch small brown leaves turn in the current, their edges softened but still recognizable. They had come from somewhere with trees.

Upstream, the channel vanished under a low stone cover. Following it would mean lying in the water, with no way to see where the covered part ended.

Still, leaves were better than more bones.

Beyond the bridge, a door stood open beneath a mark shaped like a hook. The same shape had appeared on other walls. I copied it onto the edge of my slate, hoping that enough examples might eventually mean something. Then I went through.

Beyond the door, a blue lamp hung over a signpost in the center of a round room with three other doorways. I stopped at the sight.

"I have come around in a circle?"

The left-hand doorway should lead back through the cool passage. The one behind me ought to be the slope from the broken walkway. Except I had just crossed a channel, and there had been no channel between the first round room and the walkway.

At the signpost, I found narrower boards than I remembered, one pointing toward the wall between two doorways. None of the entrances had pebbles beside it.

This was another round room.

I turned the slate toward the lamp. I had drawn the two doors in the rack room, but squeezed their lines together so tightly that the bridge appeared to belong to both. Below that, I had connected the new round room to the old one before stopping to check. I moved my thumb toward the mistaken line, then stopped. Rubbing it away meant pressing lightly against the slate. I crossed it out with a firm stroke of charcoal instead.

I knew which door I had just entered from. I no longer knew where this room was in relation to the basin. The water had turned beneath the bridge, the passage had climbed, and I had drawn all of it as though walking in a straight line.

*A map works considerably better when the person making it knows where he is.*

I sat on a low stone beside the entrance, facing the signpost, and drew a new circle in the remaining space. I marked the bridge behind me. Then I set two pebbles beside that doorway, large enough to see from the middle of the room.

Going back was still possible: across the bridge, through the racks, and on until I found the first signpost. I made myself follow that route in my head before standing up again.

The next choice could wait until I had stopped being frightened by the fact that my little drawing had lied to me.

A tap from the doorway on my right made me raise my head. Another followed, faint and uneven enough to be water dripping onto metal, or something working like the skeleton with its scraper.

Cool air moved through the doorway as I stood to listen, keeping the pebbles behind me in sight.

"Hello?"

The tapping stopped. After a while, it began again.

I drew a charcoal arrow on the floor with the same firm strokes, pointing toward the bridge, and tried the right-hand passage.

It rose through a short flight of steps and ended at a stone arch filled with smaller stones. Pale mortar stood out against the darker stone around it. A narrow gap near the top admitted the draft.

I put another cross on the slate, then looked through the gap. A lamp lit an empty walkway on the other side, with a railing along its outer edge. Beyond the railing, water reflected the blue light. I could hear it moving somewhere below.

The tapping had stopped by the time I leaned away from the gap.

"Hello?"

*I want to find someone I can actually talk to.*

With no answer, I looked down the steps toward the second round room. My pebbles were still in sight beside its entrance, so I could go back and try another route. I could also get through this.

*At least I know which side I want the stones to go toward.*

The filling beneath the arch had cracked around one stone. I checked the empty walkway again, set the slate and iron bar down, and stood to one side. With my left palm against the loose stone, I gave a light push.

The stone shot across the walkway and struck the railing with a bang that echoed over the water. The upper rail buckled outward. One of its fastenings tore from the floor, scattering chips of stone. The piece I had pushed tipped over the bent iron and vanished with a splash.

I drew my hand clear of the new hole. Two smaller stones, no longer supported by the one I had pushed out, slipped down from above. One stopped in the opening. The other struck the floor on my side of the wall, throwing dust over my remaining shoe. I stepped back before anything else fell.

I waited beside the wall until the filling stopped moving, then picked up the iron bar. Its wedge fitted beneath one of the fallen pieces. I put my shoulder behind a firm, steady push and moved it a few inches. Then I did the same with the next. The bar could carry the curse as easily as a sword. I kept each movement short, watching the stone instead of easing off in an effort to be careful.

The arch stayed in place above an opening large enough to crouch through, framing the bent railing as it leaned over the water.

For once, nobody had been urging me to push more gently or trying to kill me. I had chosen where to put my hand and opened a way forward, damaging the thing that was supposed to keep people from falling off it in the process.

*Keep clear of the railing.*

After collecting the slate and climbing through, I marked my new opening on the map and looked out over the water.

A broad reservoir spread below the railing, its surface streaked with trembling reflections from the blue lamps. Stone pillars rose through it to support beams overhead, with weights hanging from chains inside tall iron frames between them. Most were still, but one at the far end kept descending a little, catching with a metallic bang, and jerking upward again. It never completed the journey. That was the sound I had heard through several walls.

High on the opposite side, a gallery ran past closed doors, while arches lower down admitted water. Leaves turned in a slow circle where two currents met beneath a lamp. Nearer to me, a row of iron brackets showed just above the surface beside the pillars, joined by a narrow stone ledge I could make out beneath the water.

I had been imagining a dungeon that happened to contain machinery. From here, I could see how much of it had been built to move things. Water, stone, carts, people. The suspended slabs and weights were parts of a place people had needed to work.

From beside the wall, clear of the bent railing, I spotted an open door on the far gallery. A pale rag moved in the draft from its handle, above a bucket with a wooden lid. The bucket was whole, and the rag was white where the dust had not reached it.

I drew the reservoir with plenty of room around it, marking my opening on one edge and the open door opposite. Between them was water. Signs of a person were little help with a large room I could not cross between us.

Then something knocked to my right.

Three sharp sounds. A pause. Two more.

To my right, the walkway continued past three lamps and climbed four shallow steps to an arch. Its left-hand end was broken away, two bent supports marking where the rest had fallen.

The next knock came from beyond the arch.

I put the slate away and listened.

---

Water rushed inside a square wooden pipe along the wall, swelling and fading behind planks held together by iron bands. Those near my opening were rusty, but farther along one had been replaced with brighter metal.

I stopped at the new band, comparing its shape with the old ones and the lighter wood beneath it. Someone had stood right here with tools and a piece that fit, repairing something instead of abandoning it.

*They did not fly across the reservoir. There is a route.*

Beside the repair, a clear pattern of small squares marked the damp floor. I followed the boot prints to the first step, where they overlapped older marks heading the other way. People came through here more than once.

I went slowly, keeping the wall on my right and the reservoir on my left. The railing was intact beyond the section my stone had bent. I stayed away from the torn fastening and watched where the prints placed their weight.

Halfway up the steps, a chain clanked below me.

I stopped as a low, rough cry rose from far beneath the opposite gallery. It sounded like the thing below Malgorth's chamber, and ripples widened near one of the submerged pillars. I waited several breaths, but nothing else moved.

Something up here wore boots. I went through the arch in search of it before I could decide to follow the sound below.

Beyond the arch, narrow openings on the left of a covered passage still showed the water. The pipe continued along the right-hand wall and through an iron collar.

Beneath the collar, a hammer rested on a cloth beside a spool of thread, a short chisel, and a mug with a wet ring around its base. Beyond the tools, an iron gate blocked the passage beneath the next lamp.

Through its thick bars, I could see a handwheel beside the pipe and stairs climbing out of sight around a corner. A warm glow lit the ceiling above them.

Orange light.

As I stopped beside the tools, a shadow crossed the glow and a voice spoke above the turn, too quietly for me to make out.

My first impulse was to shout. I nearly did. Then I remembered the last thing that had answered me and walked to the gate instead.

"Hello?"

The voice stopped.

"I am down here," I said. "On the other side of the gate."

At the sound of footsteps above the turn, I set the iron bar on the floor and stepped back far enough to leave it in plain sight. My empty scabbard still hung at my hip. I wished I had removed that as well.

A boot and the edge of a lantern appeared on the stairs before a woman rounded the corner, holding the light away from her body.

She wore a short coat over a leather apron. Her hair was tied back beneath a cloth cap, and gray dust marked one side of her face. A heavy wrench hung from her other hand. She stopped several steps from the gate and looked at me.

She blinked against the lantern light while I stared at the mud on her boots and the annoyed crease between her eyebrows.

An actual person.

"Are you alive?" I asked.

The crease deepened.

"Yes."

I had prepared questions about the exit. I had prepared explanations for the food and the blanket. Now that someone was standing in front of me, all of them crowded together behind the much more immediate fact that she had answered.

My hands started toward the bars. I kept them at my sides and bowed my head instead. I did not trust myself to remember how I was holding something while trying to speak.

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"Answering."

She looked past me toward the arch, then back at my clothes.

"Are you bleeding?"

"Not now."

"Come closer to the light."

As I approached the gate, she raised the lantern to examine me through the bars, starting with the bandage on my arm and ending with the leather pad beneath my foot.

"Where is your other shoe?"

"A slime has it."

After considering that, she looked at the pad again.

"Those are the kneeling pads from the basin workroom."

My stomach tightened.

"Are they yours?"

"The water crews keep them there. They save our knees when we work on the pipes."

"I left a silver coin."

"For the pads?"

"And an apple. Some bread. I borrowed the blanket, but I put it back."

She lowered the lantern a little.

"You slept in the basin workroom?"

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Most of the night. I think."

Her glance over my shoulder made me turn toward the arch, but it was empty.

"What is wrong?" I asked.

"Nothing. It is a good place to stop. I am surprised you found it before something else found you."

I thought of the swords, the wolf, and the horn protruding from beneath the counterweight.

"I may have done it in the wrong order."

She set the lantern on a bracket beside the gate and tucked the wrench into a loop at her belt. The change made me realize how long I had been watching her hand.

"I brought that food down yesterday," she said. "We use the room for meals and rest between jobs. It is dry, the door can be barred, and the basin saves us carrying all our water. The blanket and tools stay there."

I thought of the bundle beside the clean iron, and the bench someone had moved to keep the door open. They had expected to come back.

"I took one piece of bread and the bruised apple."

"Then there is still something for me. You were hungry. That is what the bread is for."

"I am not working down here."

"That makes two of us who would prefer to be somewhere else."

She rubbed a dusty knuckle beneath one eye. It left another mark. She looked tired, though she was considerably better equipped for it than I was.

"My name is Haruto Minase."

"Nessa."

I repeated her name. It felt good to have one. The skeletons had not offered theirs, and I had learned Malgorth's at a time when introductions were becoming impractical.

"Do you know a way out?" I asked.

Nessa glanced up the stairs.

"Normally. The upper service stair is beyond that turn. Part of its landing fell when the old shaft came apart. I can reach the stairs, but I cannot cross the gap at the top."

"You came down here on purpose?"

"I repair the water gates. Water from the mines above drains through this reservoir. If the outlets clog, it backs up into tunnels people still use. Yesterday, I came to replace a band on a leaking pipe."

The brighter iron around the wooden pipe made sense now, along with the tools beside the wet mug.

"You finished that?"

"Yes. Then the whole gallery shook. The outlet weight caught in its guide, and the water started rising. I shut the main feed, but smaller channels are still running into the reservoir."

"Is that what you were working on?"

"I am trying to free the outlet. There is a lower inspection ledge around the reservoir, with another set of steps at the far end. Those join the service stair above the broken landing, but the water is over the ledge now. If I can get the level down, we can check whether that route is still whole."

I had seen the brackets above the water and the ledge beneath them. They had been a possible path, not another piece of decoration.

"I heard something break."

"The chains rang through the walls. The bridge outside the sorting room dropped, and the pipe started hammering every time that weight moved. It was not doing that when I arrived."

I thought of the bright scrapes on the fallen iron, the weight jerking in its frame, and the water splashing over the basin. I had noticed each one separately. Nessa had been here while the place changed.

I looked down at the silver stitches on my gloves.

She noticed.

"Were you near it?"

"Very."

"How near?"

I thought about explaining the counterweight. The obvious first question would be why I had approached a monster with four weapons. I did not have a version of the answer that improved when spoken aloud.

"There was something in a chamber. I got out."

After a moment's silence, Nessa asked, "Which chamber?"

"Bones on the floor. A weight hanging over the middle. The door closed behind me."

She stopped rubbing her eye.

"You went through the broad entrance?"

"One step."

"And came back out?"

"Eventually."

"We do not go through that entrance," she said. "The guard has never come out onto the passage while we have been working here. We stop at the basin."

I looked back toward the reservoir. I had knocked the guard under a counterweight and broken part of its doorway. Whatever the workers normally relied on, I had given Nessa a reason to check it again.

She lifted the lantern from its bracket and looked past me again, toward the passage and the reservoir beyond it. When she turned back, the crease between her eyebrows had changed.

"Was the door still closed when you left?"

"There is an opening at the bottom now."

"Did something make it?"

"Me."

Her gaze dropped to the iron bar on the floor.

"With that?"

"No. My hand."

I raised my left hand, palm toward myself. She drew back a little, and I lowered it immediately.

"I should explain that part."

"Yes."

There was no safe way to make it sound ordinary.

"I have a curse. If I push or strike something hard, almost nothing happens. If I do it gently, the result is much stronger. I can break stone."

Her eyes stayed on my hand. "Are you doing it now?"

"No. Holding things is fine. Resting my hand is fine. It starts when I push or hit."

"Can you stop yourself doing that?"

"I have been trying."

It was an honest answer. I did not like how it sounded through an iron gate.

She looked at my knees, then at the cloak, where the dust from Malgorth's chamber remained in the seams.

"Keep your hands away from the controls," she said.

"Gladly."

For a while, neither of us spoke. I waited for her to tell me to go back, or to collect the lantern and leave. She had people she worked with. She had a route she understood. I had arrived from the wrong side of a locked gate and told her that my hands broke stone.

Then she pointed at the bar.

"Leave that where it is for the moment."

"All right."

"And take a step back."

As I stepped back, she lowered the lantern to check the empty space around my feet.

"I believe you got out of that chamber," she said. "I would rather hear the rest from this side of the gate."

I let out a breath I had been holding for longer than I realized.

"You will let me through?"

"If you can walk without breaking anything."

"I can."

She did not smile, but she no longer looked as though she was deciding where to swing the wrench.

---

Nessa unhooked a bunch of keys from her belt and chose one. While she checked the gate's lower latch, I took out my slate. I wanted to show her the path I had followed. I also wanted to have something useful in my hands.

"The workroom is here," I said, pointing to the mark opposite the basin. "I went back to the junction, up these stairs, and through a room with a skeleton."

She looked up.

"Armor?"

"No. It was scraping an empty trough."

"That is the old sorting worker. Stay on the other side of the rail from it. It has never bothered us in the aisle."

"I did."

"Good. That does not apply to the ones carrying weapons."

"I learned that."

Her eyes went to the dried line on my forearm. She returned to the latch.

"How do you reach the workroom?" I asked. "I came past a wolf before I found it."

"A wolf? Where?"

"On the way toward the old lift."

"We do not use that branch. From the upper galleries, we cross the bridge outside the sorting room, take the aisle beside the trough, and go down the stairs to the junction. Then we follow the gutter to the basin."

She pointed to the broken crossing I had drawn.

"I went that way yesterday, left the food in the workroom, and came back across to repair the pipe. When the bridge fell, it cut me off from the supplies. I have not been back since."

"So you do not pass the wolf or the big chamber?"

"Neither. We keep to the marked service route. The old lift passages and the chambers beyond the basin are outside it."

I had taken the wrong branch, followed the wrong stairs, and kept walking past the place where people stopped to eat. The rooms had looked equally promising to someone who could not read their signs.

With someone else recognizing the rooms on my slate, the drawing began to look less like an account of my own mistakes.

"These two rooms look almost the same," I said. "I thought I had come around in a circle."

Nessa brought the lantern closer. I held the slate flat without pressing it against the gate.

"There are more than two," she said.

I stared at her.

"They used the same junction wherever the galleries divided."

"That seems unhelpful."

"It works better if you read the signs."

"That is another problem."

"You cannot read them?"

"No."

She looked from me to the Japanese labels.

"Those are words?"

"Yes. Just not yours."

She studied them for a moment, then pointed to the second round room.

"That one is higher than the first. You should not join them here."

"I know that now."

"And this passage slopes back toward the carts. It does not go toward the basin."

Nessa waited while I crossed out the misplaced line and drew it in the space beside it, keeping the charcoal strokes firm. Then she pointed to the opening beyond the second round room.

"That arch was filled in. How did you get through it?"

"I pushed a stone out."

She looked toward the reservoir.

"Was that the bang?"

"Yes. The stone hit the railing."

"I went up to check. I thought another weight had fallen."

"The railing is bent now. One fastening came out of the floor."

She looked at my hand, then back at the little gap on the slate.

"That gives us a way back to the basin. We keep away from that edge when we use it."

My map was still incomplete, but it was less wrong. Even that much made me want to stand straighter.

"Did you come down here alone?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Looking for something?"

"The way out. I fell through the ground."

She looked at the empty scabbard.

"Before or after you found the sword?"

"Before. The sword is somewhere under the fall you heard."

Nessa pressed her lips together.

"I have had a difficult few days," I said.

"I was beginning to suspect that."

When she straightened to try the upper latch, it stopped with a short metallic scrape. Above the hinge, a thin crack ran through the mortar, too fresh to have collected any dust.

"This opened properly yesterday," she said. "The frame has shifted."

She set the lantern back on its bracket and took out the wrench. Its flat end fitted beneath the gate's lower rail. She raised the gate a fraction while turning the key, without asking me to touch anything. The latch shifted another little way.

I waited.

Across the water, a heavy clank came from beneath the far gallery. A low cry followed it, deeper than the uneven racket of the outlet weight. Nessa stopped turning the key. We both listened until the sound faded.

"That cry," I said. "I heard it below the big chamber. Do you know what is making it?"

"I know which galleries it comes from. I do not go into them."

"Could it come up here?"

She glanced at the water.

"There are barred service gates between those galleries and the inspection passage. They were closed the last time I checked."

"That is not quite an answer."

"It is the answer I have."

I remembered the hole in Malgorth's entrance slab and the feeling of the floor moving behind me. For the first time since leaving, I wished I could be certain what I had left on the other side.

Nessa looked at me.

"Did it follow you?"

"I do not think so."

"Tell me if that changes."

She turned the key again. The bolt slid back.

I put the slate away and moved clear of the gate, steady on my feet with the cloth on my calf still dry. I was hungry, had no money, and could not know whether the flooded ledge would still be whole when we reached it.

But someone knew my name and recognized the rooms on my little map.

Nessa eased the wrench out from beneath the rail and hooked it back onto her belt. She lifted the lantern, then pulled the gate toward her. The hinges ground against each other with a long metallic groan. It carried out through the windows and over the water. We both listened until the echo faded.

"Come through. Keep to the side with the windows. Stay clear of the pipe."

I stepped across the threshold, keeping my hands close to myself. There was room beside her on the landing.

"Thank you," I said again.

This time, she nodded.

"Tell me about the chamber first."

I looked back at the water, the distant lamps, and the passage I had reached by mistake. I had been trying to find someone who could help me. Now there was someone who needed to know what I had done.

I stood beside Nessa and began at the broad doorway.
