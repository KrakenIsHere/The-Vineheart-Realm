A monster in another world died because I patted it on the head. I did not punch it, stab it, or blast it apart with legendary fire magic. I already knew a gentle touch could break stone. The problem was getting close enough to use it without losing my hand.

The wolf's front legs were pinned between two fallen beams, but its mouth still reached the rusty sword I held in front of it. Teeth closed on the blade with a scrape that traveled into my sore hand. I set my feet wider and tried not to pull back. Wrestling with it would only bring its teeth closer.

My free hand hovered above its lowered head. I could feel its breath against my wrist, and each movement of its jaw made my fingers curl. I opened them again, reached over the teeth, and rested my palm against the fur. Then I gave it the smallest nudge I could manage, away from me.

"Good doggy."

The **Hellfang Wolf** disappeared.

There was a sound like thunder trapped in a cupboard. The rotten beams burst apart as the beast tore free and went backward through a stone wall whose mortar had been falling out long before I arrived. My sword clattered at my feet. I ducked behind my raised forearm, coughing as dust rolled through the hole and chunks of stone tumbled into the chamber beyond.

When I could see again, a tuft of fur drifted past my face and settled among the rubble. My left palm was still open.

I stayed where I was until the echoes stopped. A moment earlier, the wolf's shoulders had been as high as my waist, and its teeth had looked entirely capable of removing the hand I had just used. Now I could see into the next room. It had acquired a wolf. I was in no hurry to ask whether it wanted one.

I stared at my hand. My hand stared back, offering no explanation for its behavior.

"That was supposed to stop the wolf," I whispered. *What exactly are the rules here?*

The blue window unfolded in front of my face again.

I flinched at how close it appeared, then held my hand against my chest while I read. Unlike the marks on the dungeon walls, the writing was in Japanese, just as it had been in the summoning hall. That remained its one considerate feature.

> **Unique Skill:** Inverse Impact
>
> The less physical force the bearer intentionally applies, the greater the resulting impact.
>
> **Warning:** Excessive tenderness may cause catastrophic results.

I read the warning three times. The force rule was familiar. I had tested it yesterday, at considerable expense to people who owned a training yard, and survived skeletons by using it. The part about "excessive tenderness" was new. Apparently the window had decided that breaking things was no longer embarrassing enough without a description of my technique.

"Excessive tenderness?"

A second window slid into place beneath the first.

> **Contact recorded:** Hellfang Wolf.
>
> **Hit Points:** 0 / 37.
> **Condition:** Dead.
>
> **Result:** Lethal.

"No, that is not the problem!"

My voice traveled through the broken wall. Before its echo faded, a roar came back from somewhere farther away. I covered my mouth and turned toward the hole, listening for the scrape of claws on stone.

"Sorry," I whispered. The word hardly cleared my fingers. I took a step back.

While leaning back to listen, I reached for the wall with my free hand. My fingers found a stone with crumbling mortar around its edges. I could have left my hand there. Instead, I gave it a tiny, careful push to steady myself. The stone split, and a crack ran through the loose joints above it.

I pulled away as dust sprinkled onto my shoulder. My fingers curled against my chest, and I watched the crack until the last loose grain stopped falling. Nothing else broke. I moved my feet instead of reaching for something to steady me.

*Fantastic. Even an apology requires somewhere safe to put my hands.*

The beams had bought me one chance to make contact while its teeth were occupied. I had meant to knock it away. I had no reliable way to choose how far. "Good doggy" had been an instruction to my own shoulders; dogs at home did not make me lock every muscle in my body.

There was nothing to be gained by checking the rubble. I backed out of the room, stepping around the loose stones, and found a patch of floor that had not yet developed a personal grievance against me.

I sat there until the shaking in my knees became small enough to ignore, turning my left hand over as though the dangerous part might be visible from the other side. It looked ordinary, with dirt beneath the nails and a shallow scratch across the thumb. Yesterday, that hand had held a sandwich. I would have liked to return to that level of responsibility.

Once the dust thinned, I crouched beside the doorway to retrieve the sword. My hand stopped just above the hilt when something settled beyond the broken wall. I waited, then picked it up. Two tooth marks crossed the blade and one edge had acquired a notch, but the hilt was intact. I wiped it on a clean patch of my pants before sliding it back into the scabbard.

Through the broken wall, I could see a red eye among the rubble. It remained open and motionless. I had needed the wolf to stop trying to eat me. Now that it had, I wished there were a way to choose a result somewhere between being eaten and whatever I had just done.

A pebble clicking down the rubble pile brought me to my feet, making my bruised ribs complain. Although it was only settling stone, I waited for breath, claws, or the scrape of another sword. The blue lamps made the corridor bright enough to see my own shadow, but left every doorway dark beyond its first few feet.

*One room at a time. Get through this one, then worry about the next.*

It was the sort of advice I would have resented from anyone else. Being alone had reduced the available instructors.

Testing my weight on both feet revealed that the cloth around my left sock had shifted, exposing skin to the cold floor. I sat again to fix it, holding the fabric and tucking its loose edge beneath the wrap without making anything happen. Concentrating on that small, ordinary task helped my breathing settle. There were still things I could do safely. The problem was remembering them while something with teeth argued for my attention.

---

Yesterday, I had been **Haruto Minase**, twenty-two years old, underpaid *whenever anyone was paying me*, and carrying fourteen hundred yen in my wallet when I fell asleep on a commuter train. There had been no truck, no goddess, and no heroic sacrifice. I had taken a nap, which seemed an unreasonable basis for everything that was happening.

People in robes had hoped to summon a champion. They got someone who processed invoices. My ordinary abilities had disappointed them; my 'extraordinary' skill had damaged the palace. They called what happened afterward temporary relocation. I called it being banished before dinner, though the paperwork was apparently very firm on the distinction.

Since then, I had been **robbed**, **chased** by slimes, and **dropped** into the **Hanging Halls** through a hole I had made worse while trying not to fall down it. The way back was buried. I had counted twenty-three downward stairways, then realized several might have been the same stairway approached from different directions. I stopped keeping a floor count before it became another reason to panic.

The sword at my hip was not the one the palace had given me. The bandits owned that one now. I had found this sword on a rack beside an empty guardroom, after spending several corridors insisting I did not need a weapon. Rust spotted the blade, but it had a hilt I could grip, which put it ahead of every bone I had considered carrying instead.

I also had one shoe, cloth tied around my other foot, and a phone with a cracked screen. *A miracle that the fall didn't do more damage.* The last time I checked, its battery was at eighteen percent. I had switched it off. If rescue depended on someone calling me, there were already several larger problems with the plan.

The blue windows stayed at the edge of my vision as I stepped into the corridor, following every turn of my head.

*If they know what that monster was, perhaps they know the way out.*

"Can you tell me where the exit is?"

When the writing stayed exactly as it was, I tried a request that sounded more like something a window might understand.

"Show me my status."

The wolf's assessment faded beneath a larger window bearing my name.

> **Name:** Haruto Minase.
>
> **Level:** 2.
> **Class:** None.
> **Hit Points:** 5 / 7.
> **Armor Class:** 12 (unarmored).
> **Spell Slots:** None.
>
> **Condition:** Injured, Fatigued.
> **Occupation:** None.
> **Designation:** Catastrophic Pacifist.
> **Title:** Gentle Hands.

The level and armor numbers were unchanged since the summoning hall, but my hit points had fallen from the seven I remembered before anyone started giving me things to punch. Now I had five.

I looked at the cut beneath my sleeve, then at the number. It did not tell me how many more mistakes I could survive. Five sounded like a quantity I should stop spending.

There was no class and no supply of spells tucked away somewhere. The hand that broke stone belonged to someone wearing a work shirt for protection. I had known that already. Seeing the assessment in writing made it harder to pretend the situation would improve on its own.

Then I read the new designation.

"Catastrophic Pacifist?"

The window remained politely blue.

"And please stop calling it Gentle Hands. That sounds like something you book an appointment for."

Whatever was writing these messages knew my name, recognized a monster I had never heard of, and had no apparent interest in the one piece of information that might keep me alive. A search around the window's edge revealed no customer-support option either.

The passage divided ahead. One branch climbed beneath an arch with a cracked corner. The other sloped down beside a pair of bronze rails. Both had blue lamps. Both had the same damp smell. I spent a while examining them for the kind of obvious difference a person in a story ought to notice.

There were marks cut into the wall beside the upper branch. I recognized a square, three short lines, and something shaped like a hook. They could have meant EXIT, STORAGE, or PLEASE DO NOT FEED THE WOLF. The translation spell had given me speech. It had apparently left signs as an exercise.

I set three loose pebbles in a triangle beside the junction. Carrying and placing them was harmless. I had learned enough about my hands to avoid pressing them into the floor.

The arrangement looked deliberate. If I came back, I would recognize it. If someone else came through, perhaps they would realize an intelligent creature had been here. I moved one pebble to make the triangle more even, then decided I had spent quite enough time advertising my intelligence.

I chose the upper passage. Beyond the first bend, it widened into a landing at the foot of a broad stone stairway. The stairs climbed along the right-hand wall, with cold air drifting down from above. That seemed more promising than going deeper.

A blade scraped against stone ahead of me.

Three skeletal soldiers emerged beneath a blue lamp at the far end of the landing. Rusty armor hung from their ribs, green light burned in their sockets, and all three carried swords. I had met enough skeletons by then to know they did not respond to reasonable explanations. Unfortunately, knowing what to do and keeping my hand steady while a sword came toward my face were different skills.

The first came at me with its sword raised. I drew my borrowed weapon, fumbling the last inch clear of the scabbard, and tried to bring the edge toward its skull without swinging. Then its blade filled the space in front of my face.

I flinched. My shoulder tightened, my grip locked, and the careful movement became a frantic swing before I could stop it.

The blade rang against its skull. The skull remained a skull. The skeleton tilted its head as though trying to understand the point of the demonstration.

"I meant to tap you," I said, trying to draw the blade back between us.

Its free hand came past the sword. Knuckles struck my mouth before I had room to duck, and I tasted blood. The hit-point number at the edge of my vision dropped to four.

My heel caught a fallen stone. I went backward and landed hard on my hip, losing the sword as I tried to keep my head off the floor. I reached for it, then pulled my hand back when three sets of boots entered the space in front of me. I needed air. I needed to get out from under the blades.

*I know what to do. My hand needs to stop arguing.*

Beside me stood the base of a broken statue, its carved feet all that remained at the foot of the stairs. Two skeletons approached while the third waited behind them with its sword raised. They were slow enough for me to follow the blades.

I started to reach for my sword, but it was between me and the skeleton on the right. Getting it back would mean putting my face at sword height.

The nearest blade struck the carved ankle above me with a crack. I ducked so sharply that a chip fell inside my collar, cold against the sweat at my neck. There was no time to shake it out. I crawled around the base, dragging my wrapped foot through dust and stopping for half a breath when my hip protested. The skeleton's boots followed. I moved again before its sword cleared the statue.

Its knee joint scraped the base as the second skeleton crowded behind it, trying to get around the same obstruction. Neither seemed concerned about giving the other room. I had worked beside people like that. Usually they carried coffee rather than swords.

I watched the first one's hands. When it raised the blade, its elbow left a small gap beneath the sword arm. If I could get close enough, I would only need a moment. I would also need to be right about which way the sword was going.

My left hand kept closing into a fist. I spread the fingers, rested them on my own knee, and waited for it to swing its blade. The window stayed in the corner of my vision, reporting that I was injured and fatigued. I had already gathered that information.

*Wait until it has to move its feet.*

Rubble was piled unevenly beside the base. I shifted toward it, making the skeleton change direction. The move hurt. It still felt better than giving it a clear path to my head.

I edged around the base, keeping low enough that the stone still covered my head. The first skeleton followed with its sword near its knee. Its boot caught between two stones as it stepped over the rubble, and the blade dipped while it looked down.

I brought my good foot beneath me. My hip hurt when I rose, and for a moment I was certain I would be too slow. Its forehead came within reach. I lifted my hand before it could look up.

"No hard feelings."

I flicked it.

The impact cracked through the corridor. The skull shot backward, dragging the helmet with it, and struck the skeleton immediately behind it. Armor and bones crashed into the third. By the time the pieces reached the far wall, all three skeletons had become a problem for whoever swept the floor. A helmet struck a loose brick, knocked it out, and let cold air into the passage.

Somewhere on the other side, something enormous screamed.

I dropped behind the base again, listening to the pieces strike the floor. The scream beyond the wall lasted longer than the clatter. I held my breath until it ended, then let the air out through a mouth that tasted of blood. My lip was bleeding, my hip hurt, and whatever had made that sound now knew something was happening here.

"That was a flick," I told my finger.

A treasure chest dropped from the ceiling.

It struck the floor beside the scattered bones, bounced once, and shed a cloud of dust. From behind the statue, I looked up at a storage shelf fixed high on the far wall, above the spot where the skeletons had struck. Most of it was still attached; the part that had held the chest was now beside it on the floor.

"Why was that in the ceiling?"

The latch broke off. The lid swung open, revealing a black cloak, a pair of gloves, and a silver ring. I waited for teeth, poison, or an angry little person to emerge. None did. I left the shelter of the statue and picked my way through the bones to the chest. It was already the most welcoming thing I had met underground.

When I reached toward the gloves, a new window appeared.

> **Gloves of Delicate Control**
>
> Dampens minor unintended impacts.
>
> **Deliberate force remains dangerous.**

I almost cried. Not because the warning was reassuring, but because the sentence before it contained the possibility that something might help me.

"Thank God. Any available deity of such generosity!"

Nothing exploded when I lifted the gloves by their cuffs. They were thin and dark, stitched with silver thread, and fitted over my hands once I worked off Ordan's leather pair, stiff with dirt and blood. A faint tingle moved across my knuckles, then faded.

Still nothing exploded. I sat beside the chest, with a broken crate against my back, and hugged myself in relief.

The old gloves rested in my lap. Ordan had given them to me at the palace doors with the expression of a man who knew exactly how little help they would be. One cuff was split, and dried blood had stiffened the lining. I folded them and tucked them inside my jacket. They had kept enough splinters out of my hands to deserve better than being left beside a broken chest.

The new pair fit closely enough for me to bend every finger without the leather bunching at the joints. I turned my hands beneath the lamp, following the silver stitches over my knuckles and into the seams at my wrists, looking for a buckle, a dial, or anything that would tell me how much protection I had.

The window offered the same two sentences.

*Minor unintended impacts. That is a very small promise.*

It was still a promise. Nobody at the palace had managed even that.

As I shifted to stand, I lost my balance and pushed lightly against the stone ledge beside me. The silver stitches warmed across my palm. A chip snapped off, but the rest of the ledge stayed where it was. Yesterday, a similarly careful push had broken a great deal more stone.

"Right. Less leaning."

I could hold things. I could rest my hands. Pushing to keep myself upright was a different matter, and the gloves had turned this particular mistake into a chip instead of a hole. I would have preferred a railing I could trust, but I was prepared to be grateful in installments.

The cloak smelled of cedar and old cupboards. As I lifted it from the chest, the writing changed.

> **Traveler's Cloak**
>
> Keeps the wearer dry and improves personal insulation.

Wrapping it around myself made the draft against my back considerably less interesting. When I picked up the silver ring, another window appeared.

> **Ring of Mild Warmth**
>
> Provides a steady, gentle warmth to the wearer.
>
> **Does not prevent hypothermia.**

I slid it onto my right index finger. Warmth spread through my cold hand, faint but welcome. Neither window promised an exit, a meal, or replacement footwear. I kept them anyway. At least they had a purpose I understood.

*It is not cold enough for me to worry about hypothermia anyway. But staying warm is a plus.*

With the new equipment on, I walked back to the statue at the foot of the stairs. My sword was still where I had dropped it during the fight. I retrieved it, checked that the scabbard had not come loose, and started up, leaving the chest and the skeletons' remains.

At the top of the stairs, I stopped on a stone walkway overlooking a square shaft. The stairs were behind me; ahead, an iron railing guarded the drop except for a doorway-sized gap beneath a blue lamp.

Immediately beyond the gap, a wooden platform sat inside the shaft with its floor level with the walkway. Its low iron fence had an open gate facing me, close enough for me to step straight onto it. I stayed on the stone.

Two thick iron rails climbed the shaft walls, one on either side of the platform. A heavy chain attached to the platform disappeared into the dark overhead. To its right, a long stone weight hung from a second chain, inside a narrow channel in the wall. Grooves marked where the weight had scraped its guides. Through the space between the platform and the walkway, I could see the shaft continuing down into darkness. This was some kind of goods lift.

A handwheel and a lever were fixed to the railing on the right of the opening, within reach from the walkway. Three marks above the lever were writing. I studied them for several seconds, hoping one would turn into something useful through the strength of my need. They remained marks.

High up the shaft, light shone through a break in the stonework. For a moment, the pale patch looked like sky, but leaning sideways changed the angle enough to reveal its glass cage. Another blue lamp.

*Maybe the platform could get me closer to the surface.*

I moved beside the handwheel and examined the mechanism. A short metal shaft connected it to a large toothed gear, with a catch lodged between two teeth. The rest of the machinery disappeared behind an iron cover. I had no idea what the lever did. I assumed the catch was keeping the platform from dropping at an overly exciting speed.

A dusty painted board rested on the walkway beside the controls. Holding it by the edges beneath the lamp over the gate, I could make out one figure at the wheel and another inside the platform with a load of boxes. Two people.

I set the board down. *Was there a way to use the thing alone?* Perhaps the lever locked the platform at each landing. Perhaps there was another control inside it. I probably couldn't investigate without getting in, or stop it if anything started moving in a way I disliked.

The rim of the handwheel was cold. I closed both hands around it and tried a firm, steady turn, the same sort of pressure that had moved a board normally in the training yard. The mechanism resisted, then scraped through a short movement. Above me, the chain tightened, and the platform rose a few inches. When I stopped, it stayed where it was.

I was beginning to think this might work when something clicked beneath it.

A narrow strip of metal came free beneath the platform. I stopped turning the wheel and watched it fall past the blue light. For a while there was no sound. I had time to imagine standing where that piece had been.

When it finally struck something below, I took my hands off the wheel and stepped back from the opening. The catch held. The stone beneath my feet was solid. I stayed there until I could look at the platform without wanting to move farther away.

Crouching on the walkway let me see beneath the platform's raised edge, where two iron supports were bent and a third had pulled partly out of its joint.

I returned to the railing and leaned just far enough to look up the shaft. The rails continued past the higher lamp, but the landing beside it was choked with rubble. There was no clear opening, and no way to tell whether the platform would reach it before its remaining supports gave up.

I waited a little longer, as though a better answer might become visible. Then I left the platform where it was and stepped away from the controls.

I went back down the stairs, past the fallen chest and the remains of the skeletons. I kept looking over my shoulder. Leaving something that might have been an exit felt worse than finding a wall. But the platform had lost a piece before I put my weight on it. My skill could not hold its floor together, and the gloves would do very little if I fell through it.

I followed the passage back to the junction. My three pebbles were still arranged beside the arch. I added a fourth across the upper side of the triangle. It would remind me that I had tried that way.

The lower passage had water running along it. I could hear it now that things had gone quiet.
